Phase I oncology clinical-trial documentation: end-to-end operating model

Scope

The following is a comprehensive inventory and workflow for interventional Phase I oncology drug or biologic trials, including first-in-human dose escalation and expansion cohorts. It uses ICH requirements as the international baseline and U.S. IND requirements as concrete regulatory examples. Country-specific clinical-trial applications, privacy documents, insurance documents, and ethics forms must be added for each jurisdiction.

No regulator prescribes one universal corporate writing workflow. Regulations and guidance specify responsibilities, document content, data integrity, reviewability, reporting deadlines, and records that must be retained. Sponsors operationalize those requirements through controlled templates, authoring plans, review matrices, quality-control procedures, electronic document-management systems, and trial master files [1][2][6][25][26]. ICH E6(R3) now explicitly expects protocols and related plans—including the statistical analysis, data-management, and monitoring plans—to be clear, concise, operationally feasible, risk-proportionate, traceable, and fit for purpose [1][2].  ￼

“Large documents” below means controlled documents that integrate substantial scientific, clinical, operational, statistical, safety, regulatory, or patient-level information. Numerous smaller essential records—licenses, signature pages, correspondence, temperature logs, delegation records, training certificates, query forms, and individual safety forms—feed those documents but are not each treated as a separate large document.

1. Who actually creates the documents

The principal investigator and medical writer rarely create a global trial document alone.

Role	Primary documentation responsibility
Sponsor clinical lead or study physician	Owns the clinical-development rationale, protocol strategy, dose-escalation rules, medical interpretation, eligibility rules, benefit–risk decisions, and major clinical conclusions.
Site principal investigator	Owns medical care and oversight at the site; source records; eligibility confirmation; consent oversight; causality and seriousness assessments; protocol-deviation assessment; timely eCRF and safety reporting; and investigator endorsement of site data.
Medical writer	Creates the document plan and shell, integrates source material, drafts narratives, reconciles terminology across documents, manages review comments, checks internal consistency, and prepares an approval-ready document. The writer does not independently invent clinical conclusions or statistical results.
Biostatistician	Owns design calculations, analysis populations, statistical methods, SAP, shells, interpretation of statistical outputs, and approval of statistical text.
Statistical programmer	Produces validated analysis datasets, tables, figures, listings, and traceability documentation.
Clinical data manager	Designs eCRFs, data standards and edit checks; writes the Data Management Plan; manages queries, coding, reconciliation, cleaning, freeze, and lock.
Pharmacovigilance physician/scientist	Owns safety case processing, expectedness, aggregate safety review, safety narratives, Reference Safety Information, expedited reports, and DSUR safety sections.
Clinical pharmacologist/PK scientist	Owns sampling strategy, PK/PD analysis plans, concentration data review, exposure-response analyses, and PK reports.
Translational/biomarker scientist	Owns biomarker hypotheses, assay plans, specimen requirements, analysis methods, exploratory results, and translational reports.
Clinical operations lead/CRA	Owns site feasibility and activation materials, monitoring plans and reports, operational tracking, protocol-compliance oversight, and site closeout.
Regulatory affairs	Determines the submission strategy, required regional components, submission sequence and metadata; manages health-authority questions, amendments, annual reports, and eCTD publication.
Quality assurance/document QC	Performs process audits and independent content, numerical, consistency, formatting, and compliance QC.
Regulatory publishing	Converts approved documents into submission-ready PDFs or structured content, adds bookmarks, hyperlinks and metadata, validates files, and assembles the eCTD sequence.
IRB/IEC and health authorities	Review rather than author sponsor documents, but their questions and conditions cause revisions.
Sponsor-investigator	In an investigator-initiated IND, assumes both sponsor and investigator obligations and normally delegates writing, statistics, data management, safety, and publishing to institutional or contracted specialists.

The investigator remains responsible for adequate source records and for the accuracy, completeness, legibility, and timeliness of data sent to the sponsor, including CRFs and SAE reports [1][3][4]. The sponsor remains responsible for global data management, analysis, quality management, regulatory reporting, and preparation of clinical-trial reports [1].  ￼

2. The common production method for every large document

Although the content changes, most large Phase I documents are created through the same controlled sequence.

Step 1 — Establish document ownership and purpose

The team creates a document plan defining:
	•	regulatory or operational purpose;
	•	accountable owner and medical/statistical approvers;
	•	lead writer;
	•	required template and governing standard;
	•	source documents and data cutoff;
	•	section-level contributors;
	•	review rounds and reviewers;
	•	QC requirements;
	•	approval signatures;
	•	destination—IRB/IEC, health authority, eTMF, investigator-site file, registry, journal, or internal governance body.

ICH M11, finalized by FDA in May 2026, provides a harmonized protocol structure, template, and technical specification. Industry template suites also align protocol, SAP, and CSR content so definitions can be authored once and reused downstream [6][26].  ￼

Step 2 — Establish the authoritative source package

Before drafting, the owner identifies the controlled inputs. Depending on the document, these may include:
	•	development plan and target product profile;
	•	current Investigator’s Brochure;
	•	nonclinical pharmacology and toxicology reports;
	•	CMC summaries;
	•	protocol and amendments;
	•	prior health-authority advice;
	•	finalized SAP;
	•	Data Management Plan;
	•	eCRF and annotated CRF;
	•	coding dictionaries;
	•	locked or cutoff datasets;
	•	validated tables, figures and listings;
	•	PK, biomarker, imaging, safety, and laboratory outputs;
	•	dose-escalation committee decisions;
	•	protocol-deviation and quality-issue listings;
	•	prior safety reports;
	•	literature-search results;
	•	patient-level case information for narratives.

The document plan states the exact version and cutoff date of each source. Drafting from uncontrolled spreadsheets, copied email figures, or superseded protocol language is avoided.

Step 3 — Create the document shell and content map

The medical writer or functional author builds a controlled shell containing:
	•	title, protocol number, product, phase, version and date;
	•	confidentiality and disclosure statements;
	•	document history;
	•	authors, reviewers and approvers;
	•	table of contents;
	•	predefined headings;
	•	abbreviation and terminology conventions;
	•	planned tables, figures, listings and appendices;
	•	section owners and drafting notes;
	•	links to reused protocol or SAP content.

For data-driven reports, the shell is normally developed before database lock. “Dummy” tables and figure placeholders show exactly which outputs will populate each conclusion.

Step 4 — Draft from controlled evidence

Each specialist supplies approved text or data. The writer:
	1.	imports controlled reusable content;
	2.	summarizes rather than recopies unnecessarily;
	3.	connects objectives to endpoints, collection methods, analysis methods, results and conclusions;
	4.	keeps patient identifiers coded;
	5.	uses the protocol-specified terminology and assessment standards;
	6.	ties every number to a validated table, listing, dataset or source report;
	7.	marks assumptions or unavailable results;
	8.	records unresolved questions in an author query log.

Medical writers generally draft from approved aggregate outputs and controlled listings, not by independently analyzing raw EHR data. Patient narratives are an exception: the writer may need the safety case, CRF data, laboratory history, dosing history and site clarification, but the investigator and safety physician validate the medical facts and interpretation.

Step 5 — Cross-functional review

A typical review sequence is:
	1.	Core-team review: clinical lead, writer, statistician, data manager, safety, PK and operations.
	2.	Specialist review: biomarkers, imaging, CMC, nonclinical, quality, legal/privacy, or regional regulatory experts.
	3.	Senior governance review: development team, safety committee, protocol review committee, or submission governance.
	4.	Final approver review: accountable physician, statistician, regulatory lead and, where required, coordinating investigator.

Comments are collected in a controlled system or comment matrix. The writer resolves each comment, documents the disposition, prevents conflicting edits, and escalates scientific disagreements to the accountable owner.

Step 6 — Independent QC

QC normally contains several separate checks:
	•	Data QC: every number agrees with the approved output.
	•	Medical consistency QC: subject histories, diagnoses, exposure, AEs and conclusions agree across the narrative, tables and listings.
	•	Cross-document QC: objectives, endpoints, populations, dose terminology and assessment schedules agree with the protocol, SAP, ICF, registry record and CSR.
	•	Editorial QC: terminology, abbreviations, grammar and defined terms are consistent.
	•	Technical QC: pagination, bookmarks, hyperlinks, cross-references, appendices, PDF properties and file names work.
	•	Privacy QC: direct identifiers and disclosive information are removed or handled under the applicable disclosure rules.
	•	Submission QC: correct eCTD location, metadata, lifecycle operation and validation status.

Statistical programming and analysis require documented QC and traceability from the reported result through analysis data to collected data [1][13][14][15][23].  ￼

Step 7 — Approval, publication and filing

The final clean version is approved using controlled electronic or physical signatures. Essential records should identify the author, reviewer, approver, date, version and—where necessary—signature [1]. Approved records are published for their intended use and filed contemporaneously in the sponsor TMF and/or investigator-site file [1][25]. FDA-regulated electronic records and signatures must satisfy applicable Part 11 controls, including trustworthiness, reliability, integrity and inspection availability [16].  ￼

A. Collection of data and patient information before, during and after the Phase I trial

A1. Before the first participant is enrolled

1. Define what must be collected

The protocol team starts with each objective and endpoint and works backward:
	1.	What decision will the endpoint support?
	2.	Which variables are needed?
	3.	At what visits or time points?
	4.	From which source?
	5.	Who assesses or enters it?
	6.	Which standard, unit or dictionary applies?
	7.	How will it be analyzed?
	8.	Which deviations or missing values would make it unusable?

For an oncology Phase I trial, the pretrial design must address starting-dose justification, escalation method, DLT definition and window, stopping rules, dose modifications, expansion cohorts, PK/PD sampling, preliminary activity, and the evidence needed to select an optimized dose—not merely the highest tolerated dose [7][8][9].  ￼

2. Build a source-data map

Before activation, the sponsor and site define which record is the authoritative source for every important variable. Examples include:
	•	EHR physician and nursing notes;
	•	pathology and molecular-testing reports;
	•	radiology images and reports;
	•	local and central laboratory systems;
	•	pharmacy and investigational-product records;
	•	infusion records;
	•	electronic consent system;
	•	ePRO system;
	•	ECG vendor;
	•	central imaging review platform;
	•	sample-management and biorepository systems;
	•	PK and biomarker laboratories.

ICH E6(R3) expects the investigator to define source records, methods of capture and their locations before the trial and to avoid unnecessary transcription [1].  ￼

3. Design the eCRF and data standards

The data manager converts the protocol into:
	•	eCRF forms and field specifications;
	•	controlled terminology;
	•	field-level completion instructions;
	•	edit checks;
	•	derived-field rules;
	•	visit windows;
	•	missing-data conventions;
	•	external vendor transfer specifications;
	•	medical coding rules;
	•	SDTM mapping specifications;
	•	aCRF annotations;
	•	data-review listings.

The team aims to collect only data needed for participant safety, protocol execution, analysis, regulatory reporting or future development decisions. Data collected “just in case” increase site burden and create more opportunities for inconsistency [1][2].

4. Collect screening and baseline data

Common Phase I oncology screening information includes:
	•	consent version, date, time and person conducting consent;
	•	coded screening and subject identifiers;
	•	age, sex and protocol-required demographic variables;
	•	cancer diagnosis, histology, stage and disease sites;
	•	pathology confirmation and relevant molecular alterations;
	•	measurable or evaluable disease;
	•	prior systemic therapy, radiotherapy, surgery and investigational treatment;
	•	dates, best responses, progression and reasons for stopping prior treatment;
	•	washout periods;
	•	comorbidities and clinically relevant medical history;
	•	baseline signs and symptoms;
	•	ECOG or other performance status;
	•	physical examination, vital signs, weight and body surface area;
	•	hematology, chemistry, coagulation, urinalysis and organ-function results;
	•	ECG and cardiac testing where applicable;
	•	baseline imaging and target/non-target lesions;
	•	concomitant medications;
	•	reproductive status and pregnancy testing where applicable;
	•	archival or fresh tumor tissue availability;
	•	infectious-disease testing where required;
	•	eligibility checklist, investigator confirmation and screen-failure reason.

Consent must occur before trial participation, be documented in the case history, use the currently approved form, and be updated or repeated when material new information could affect willingness to continue [1][3][5].  ￼

Direct identifiers ordinarily remain in site-controlled records. Sponsor systems normally use a coded trial identifier; the subject-identification linkage is maintained under restricted site access.

A2. During treatment and dose escalation

1. Site-level clinical capture

At each visit, qualified site personnel document in source records and then enter or transmit protocol-required data concerning:
	•	treatment date, cycle, day and actual dose;
	•	route, infusion start/stop, interruptions and infusion reactions;
	•	dose calculation and body-size inputs;
	•	drug lot or kit and investigational-product accountability;
	•	missed, delayed, reduced or held doses and reason;
	•	concomitant medications and supportive treatment;
	•	examinations, performance status, vital signs and ECGs;
	•	local and central laboratory values;
	•	PK collection time relative to dosing and actual sample time;
	•	PD, tumor, blood or other biospecimen collection and processing;
	•	adverse-event onset, resolution, grade, seriousness, action taken, outcome and causality;
	•	dose-limiting toxicities;
	•	hospitalizations and emergency visits;
	•	pregnancy information;
	•	protocol deviations;
	•	tumor measurements and overall response;
	•	patient-reported outcomes, when included;
	•	treatment-discontinuation reason.

For U.S. IND trials, an investigator must immediately report every SAE to the sponsor, whether or not considered drug-related, with an assessment of whether the drug may have caused it; nonserious AEs are recorded and reported according to the protocol [4].  ￼

2. Oncology assessment conventions

The protocol defines the exact grading and response criteria. CTCAE supplies standardized adverse-event terms and grades; CTCAE v6.0 was released in 2025, although an ongoing protocol must continue to use its specified version unless formally amended [10]. Solid-tumor response is generally collected using RECIST 1.1; immunotherapy trials may additionally use iRECIST when prospectively specified [11][12].  ￼

The trial does not silently change grading dictionaries, RECIST conventions, laboratory normal ranges or coding versions during conduct. Version changes require documented impact assessment, mapping, validation and, where material, amendment or analysis-plan revision.

3. Data flow and cleaning

A typical flow is:

Source record → eCRF or validated vendor system → automated edit checks → central data review → site query → investigator correction/confirmation → coding and reconciliation → SDTM → ADaM → tables, figures and listings.

Parallel reconciliations include:
	•	clinical database versus safety database;
	•	EDC exposure versus pharmacy accountability;
	•	EDC sample records versus central laboratory data;
	•	site response versus central imaging review;
	•	death data versus SAE and survival records;
	•	protocol deviations versus analysis exclusions;
	•	informed-consent dates versus first trial procedure;
	•	randomization or enrollment data versus EDC subject status.

Changes remain attributable and traceable; the original value is not obscured [1][16].

4. Dose-escalation decision data

After each protocol-defined DLT observation window, the data team prepares a dose-review package. It normally contains:
	•	enrollment and evaluability by cohort;
	•	actual dose and exposure;
	•	DLT table with medical descriptions;
	•	cumulative TEAE and treatment-related AE tables;
	•	grade 3–5 events and serious events;
	•	deaths and discontinuations;
	•	clinically important laboratory, ECG and vital-sign trends;
	•	dose interruptions and reductions;
	•	PK concentration and exposure summaries;
	•	available PD and biomarker data;
	•	preliminary tumor-response data;
	•	unresolved data queries and protocol deviations;
	•	model output, if a model-based escalation design is used;
	•	recommended action and alternatives.

The Safety Review Committee, Dose Escalation Committee or similar body reviews the package and documents the decision to escalate, de-escalate, backfill, expand, modify the schedule, pause or stop. Committee charters should define membership, quorum, data cutoff, decision algorithm, conflict management, voting, emergency meetings, minutes and communication procedures [1][8].

⸻

A3. After treatment and after trial completion

Post-treatment collection commonly includes:
	•	protocol-defined safety follow-up;
	•	unresolved AE and SAE outcome;
	•	new anticancer treatment;
	•	disease progression;
	•	subsequent response where relevant;
	•	survival status and date last known alive;
	•	cause of death;
	•	pregnancy outcome;
	•	delayed or immune-mediated toxicity;
	•	long-term laboratory or organ effects;
	•	sample disposition or permitted future use;
	•	end-of-treatment and end-of-study assessments;
	•	final investigational-product reconciliation;
	•	withdrawal of consent and scope of withdrawal;
	•	lost-to-follow-up attempts;
	•	final investigator data review and eCRF endorsement.

The investigator supplies a final report to the sponsor shortly after completing participation [4]. The site and sponsor then reconcile essential records, resolve outstanding data and safety discrepancies, close the site, and retain the records for the applicable period [1][3][25].  ￼

B. Large documents created before the Phase I trial

B1. Clinical-development and regulatory strategy documents

1. Clinical Development Plan

Lead: development lead and clinical lead; medical writer often structures it.

Inputs: mechanism, nonclinical evidence, unmet need, competitive landscape, regulatory advice, biomarker strategy, CMC readiness and commercial objectives.

Creation method: The team defines the intended population, sequence of studies, decision criteria, evidence gaps, dose strategy, combinations, biomarker hypotheses, pediatric considerations, registration path and risks. The Phase I protocol is then derived from this broader plan.

2. Phase I design and dose-rationale memorandum

Lead: clinical pharmacology, toxicology, clinical lead and biostatistics.

It documents:
	•	nonclinical starting-dose calculations;
	•	anticipated therapeutic and toxic exposure;
	•	species selection and toxicology findings;
	•	pharmacologically active exposure;
	•	escalation increments;
	•	sentinel dosing, if used;
	•	DLT period;
	•	stopping boundaries;
	•	model assumptions;
	•	expansion triggers;
	•	evidence required to recommend a dose.

For conventional oncology products, ICH S9 frames the nonclinical package supporting trials in patients with advanced cancer and limited treatment options [7]. Modern dose development should integrate safety, tolerability, PK, PD and activity rather than assume that the maximum tolerated dose is always the optimal dose [9].

3. Regulatory interaction package

Examples include pre-IND, scientific-advice, INTERACT or equivalent meeting packages.

Creation method: Regulatory affairs creates the question strategy; functions write concise background sections; the writer integrates the briefing book; senior governance approves each question and sponsor position. It includes product background, nonclinical and CMC summaries, protocol synopsis, proposed starting dose and escalation, specific questions, sponsor position and supporting evidence.

4. Initial IND/CTA dossier

Typical integrated components include:
	•	administrative and regional forms;
	•	overall investigational plan;
	•	Investigator’s Brochure;
	•	clinical protocol;
	•	CMC information;
	•	pharmacology and toxicology summaries and reports;
	•	previous human experience;
	•	investigator and site information;
	•	environmental or regional statements;
	•	cross-references to master files;
	•	electronic submission metadata.

Regulatory affairs compiles the dossier. Functional experts own their modules; the medical writer often authors clinical or nonclinical summaries; publishing assembles and validates the electronic sequence.

⸻

B2. Core clinical documents

5. Investigator’s Brochure

Lead: medical writer or safety writer under the sponsor physician.

Inputs: physical/chemical properties, formulation, nonclinical pharmacology and toxicology, PK, prior clinical exposure, safety cases, class information and risk-management recommendations.

How it is created:
	1.	Create an evidence cutoff.
	2.	Summarize nonclinical and human findings by study.
	3.	integrate cumulative exposure and safety.
	4.	identify known and potential risks.
	5.	create Reference Safety Information where applicable.
	6.	give investigators practical guidance for recognition, treatment and reporting.
	7.	conduct medical, nonclinical, CMC, safety and regulatory review.
	8.	approve, distribute and document investigator receipt.

ICH E6 expects the brochure to give investigators sufficient understanding of possible risks, adverse reactions, precautions and treatment of overdose or reactions [1].

6. Phase I clinical protocol

Lead: sponsor clinical lead; medical writer manages integrated drafting.

ICH M11 and ICH E6 are used to create a harmonized, operationally feasible structure [1][6]. The writer generally conducts protocol-authoring meetings and maintains a decision log.

The protocol includes:
	•	synopsis and schedule of activities;
	•	background and rationale;
	•	known and potential risks and benefits;
	•	objectives, estimands where relevant, and endpoints;
	•	study design and overall schema;
	•	dose-escalation algorithm;
	•	starting dose and increment justification;
	•	DLT definition, evaluability and observation window;
	•	intrapatient escalation rules;
	•	cohort replacement rules;
	•	backfill and expansion rules;
	•	stopping and pausing rules;
	•	MTD, MAD, RP2D or optimized-dose definitions;
	•	population and eligibility;
	•	treatment administration;
	•	dose modifications;
	•	prohibited and permitted concomitant treatment;
	•	safety assessments and reporting;
	•	PK/PD and biomarker sampling;
	•	tumor-response assessments;
	•	statistics and sample-size rationale;
	•	data handling and record retention;
	•	monitoring and quality management;
	•	ethics, consent and confidentiality;
	•	committee governance;
	•	publication and disclosure policy;
	•	appendices and referenced manuals.

For expansion cohorts, each cohort’s population, objective, sample size, stopping rule, safety monitoring, PK and activity analysis should be explicit [8].

7. Protocol synopsis and study schema

These are controlled derivatives of the protocol, not independent summaries. The writer extracts the design, population, treatment, objectives, endpoints and critical timelines and then performs a line-by-line consistency check against the approved protocol.

8. Informed Consent Form and patient-facing materials

Lead: medical writer, clinical team, legal/privacy and regional regulatory specialists; PI and IRB/IEC review site suitability.

The ICF is built from the protocol and IB but written for the intended patient population. It covers:
	•	research purpose and voluntary nature;
	•	procedures and duration;
	•	dose escalation and uncertainty;
	•	foreseeable risks and discomforts;
	•	reproductive risks;
	•	alternatives;
	•	potential benefits without overstating efficacy;
	•	injury and compensation language;
	•	confidentiality and data access;
	•	biospecimen and genetic research;
	•	future use of data or samples;
	•	costs and payments;
	•	stopping and withdrawal;
	•	new information;
	•	contacts and complaints;
	•	signatures and dates.

A consent cross-reference matrix can map every protocol procedure and risk to the corresponding ICF language. Readability, translation, back-translation where required, legal review, IRB/IEC approval and version control occur before use [5].

9. Recruitment, prescreening and optional-consent materials

These include advertisements, prescreening scripts, caregiver information, optional biopsy consent, genetic testing consent and future-use consent. Each is checked to avoid therapeutic misconception, undue influence and statements inconsistent with the protocol or ICF.

B3. Data, statistics and safety documents

10. eCRF specification, annotated CRF and completion guidelines

Lead: data management.

The CRF is derived through a protocol-to-data matrix. Every field should support an endpoint, safety obligation, eligibility criterion, operational decision or required regulatory variable. Specifications define field type, format, units, codelist, edit checks, derivation status and SDTM destination.

11. Data Management Plan

The DMP describes:
	•	database design and validation;
	•	user roles;
	•	source and data-flow architecture;
	•	data entry and query rules;
	•	external data transfers;
	•	coding dictionaries;
	•	SAE reconciliation;
	•	laboratory and imaging reconciliation;
	•	deviation handling;
	•	review listings and metrics;
	•	freeze and lock criteria;
	•	post-lock changes;
	•	archiving and transfer.

12. Statistical Analysis Plan

Lead: biostatistician.

It is consistent with the protocol and finalizes:
	•	analysis populations;
	•	DLT-evaluable rules;
	•	safety and activity definitions;
	•	dose-escalation model or algorithm;
	•	handling of partial follow-up and replacement subjects;
	•	baseline definition;
	•	treatment-emergent windows;
	•	missing data;
	•	PK and exposure-response summaries;
	•	response and duration endpoints;
	•	subgroup analyses;
	•	protocol deviations;
	•	interim analyses;
	•	table, figure and listing specifications.

The SAP should be finalized before the relevant analysis and any unblinding. Deviations from planned analyses must be documented and justified [1].

13. TFL shells and mock dose-review outputs

Biostatistics and medical writing design shells before final data are available. This exposes ambiguities in endpoint definitions and ensures that the CRF collects everything needed for dose decisions and the CSR.

14. Safety Management or Safety Surveillance Plan

Lead: pharmacovigilance.

It defines:
	•	SAE intake and triage;
	•	follow-up attempts;
	•	MedDRA coding;
	•	seriousness, expectedness and causality;
	•	unblinding rules;
	•	AESIs;
	•	aggregate signal review;
	•	clinical/safety database reconciliation;
	•	expedited-report decision making;
	•	investigator notification;
	•	safety-governance meetings;
	•	annual aggregate reporting;
	•	responsibilities and timelines.

15. Pharmacovigilance forms and narrative templates

The team creates SAE forms, pregnancy forms, special-situation forms, follow-up questionnaires and standard narrative structures before first patient first visit.

⸻

B4. Operational and technical manuals

16. Monitoring Plan and central-monitoring plan

These define site-monitoring frequency, source-data review strategy, critical data, risk indicators, escalation thresholds, visit reports, issue management and closeout requirements.

17. Quality Management Plan and trial risk assessment

The team identifies critical-to-quality factors—such as consent, eligibility, correct dose, timely SAE reporting, DLT completeness, PK sample timing and reliable tumor assessment—and defines controls, metrics, thresholds, escalation and CAPA processes [1][2].

18. Pharmacy or Investigational Product Manual

It contains receipt, storage, preparation, dispensing, dosing, accountability, temperature management, excursions, returns, destruction, blinding and occupational-safety instructions.

19. Laboratory, PK and biospecimen manuals

These specify collection tubes, timing, processing, centrifugation, aliquoting, labeling, shipment, chain of custody, stability, deviations and data transfer.

20. Imaging manual or independent-review charter

It defines modality, acquisition parameters, lesion selection, assessment schedule, RECIST/iRECIST conventions, reader training, adjudication and data-transfer rules [11][12].

21. Dose-review/Safety Review Committee charter

It establishes membership, quorum, data reviewed, DLT decision process, meeting timing, voting, urgent review, documentation and communication.

22. Independent Data Monitoring Committee charter

Not every Phase I trial uses an independent DMC. When used, its charter defines independence, closed/open sessions, unblinded access, statistical outputs, recommendation options and record retention.

23. TMF plan and essential-record index

The sponsor assigns each record an owner, expected location, naming convention, filing deadline, metadata, quality check and retention rule. The site creates the corresponding investigator-site file structure. Essential records must permit reconstruction and evaluation of trial conduct and results [1][25].

24. Training and site-initiation package

This incorporates protocol training, DLT examples, safety reporting, consent process, pharmacy procedures, data entry, sample processing, imaging requirements and escalation contacts. Completion and comprehension are documented.

C. Large documents created during the Phase I trial

C1. Protocol amendments and associated document cascade

A protocol amendment begins with a documented change request arising from new safety data, operational difficulty, health-authority feedback, emerging PK/PD information, expansion-cohort activation or a dose-strategy change.

The authoring sequence is:
	1.	document rationale and urgency;
	2.	assess safety, scientific, statistical, operational and regulatory impact;
	3.	revise the protocol with tracked changes;
	4.	revise the synopsis and schedule of activities;
	5.	update the ICF and patient materials;
	6.	update IB, SAP, CRFs, edit checks, manuals, registry record and training;
	7.	conduct cross-document consistency QC;
	8.	obtain sponsor approval;
	9.	submit to authorities and IRB/IEC as applicable;
	10.	distribute and train;
	11.	reconsent affected participants where required;
	12.	file approvals, versions and implementation evidence.

No site implements a non-urgent amendment before required approval. Immediate hazard-mitigation changes are documented and reported under the applicable rules.

C2. Updated Investigator’s Brochure and Reference Safety Information

Safety, nonclinical and clinical teams perform periodic and event-driven review. New cumulative data are assessed for:
	•	new expected reactions;
	•	changed frequency or severity;
	•	new risk factors;
	•	revised monitoring;
	•	contraindications;
	•	changed dose-modification guidance;
	•	new nonclinical findings;
	•	changes requiring protocol or ICF revision.

The writer updates the cumulative sections and change summary; pharmacovigilance owns RSI decisions; regulatory affairs manages submission and investigator distribution.

C3. Dose-escalation and cohort-review packages

For every scheduled review, data management and programming apply a predefined cutoff and generate current outputs. The medical monitor prepares a recommendation, and the writer or operations lead assembles:
	•	enrollment and exposure;
	•	DLTs and evaluability;
	•	cumulative safety;
	•	deaths and discontinuations;
	•	PK/PD;
	•	early activity;
	•	deviations and data-quality limitations;
	•	prior decision history;
	•	proposed next step.

Minutes record attendees, cutoff, conflicts, data discussed, decision, rationale, conditions and actions. These minutes are essential records; they are not replaced by informal email.

C4. Expedited individual safety reports

The process is:
	1.	site submits the SAE and investigator causality assessment;
	2.	safety personnel enter and code the case;
	3.	missing clinically important information is queried;
	4.	the sponsor evaluates seriousness, expectedness and reasonable possibility of causality;
	5.	reportability and clock start are determined;
	6.	the report and narrative are medically reviewed;
	7.	it is submitted to authorities and investigators as applicable;
	8.	follow-up information is reported and reconciled with EDC.

Under U.S. IND rules, reportable serious and unexpected suspected adverse reactions are generally due within 15 calendar days; unexpected fatal or life-threatening suspected reactions are due within 7 calendar days [17].  ￼

As of April 1, 2026, serious and unexpected suspected adverse-reaction reports for commercial INDs under 21 CFR 312.32(c)(1)(i) must be sent electronically to FDA’s AEMS using E2B(R3), either database-to-database or through the Safety Reporting Portal. Noncommercial INDs, including investigator-sponsored INDs, are exempt from that particular electronic requirement. Other categories of IND safety reports continue through eCTD [18].  ￼

C5. DSUR and U.S. IND Annual Report

DSUR

The DSUR integrates worldwide development safety information for a defined reporting interval:
	•	executive summary;
	•	worldwide marketing authorization status;
	•	regulatory actions for safety reasons;
	•	reference safety information;
	•	inventory of ongoing and completed trials;
	•	cumulative exposure;
	•	interval line listings and summary tabulations;
	•	significant clinical and nonclinical findings;
	•	literature;
	•	lack of efficacy where relevant to serious disease;
	•	region-specific information;
	•	signal evaluation;
	•	integrated benefit–risk assessment;
	•	important risks and planned actions [19].  ￼

U.S. IND annual report

Under 21 CFR 312.33, the sponsor submits it within 60 days of the IND anniversary. It contains each study’s status and enrollment, available results, AE summaries, prior IND safety reports, deaths, AE-associated dropouts, dose-response or pharmacologic findings, nonclinical findings, manufacturing changes, future investigational plan, IB revisions and significant Phase I protocol modifications [20].  ￼

Sponsors often create one harmonized data package for DSUR and annual-report components, while regulatory affairs ensures each regional legal requirement is met.

C6. Interim analysis or interim clinical study report

An interim CSR may be needed for:
	•	major regulatory meetings;
	•	transition to expansion;
	•	breakthrough or expedited-program interactions;
	•	partnership diligence;
	•	a new combination strategy;
	•	a major safety decision;
	•	support for another protocol or application.

The data cutoff, maturity, unresolved queries and limitations must be stated prominently. It follows relevant ICH E3 principles but is clearly labeled interim [1][21].

C7. Monitoring, quality and compliance reports

Major controlled reports during conduct include:
	•	site initiation report;
	•	routine monitoring visit report;
	•	remote monitoring report;
	•	centralized monitoring report;
	•	risk review report;
	•	protocol-deviation trend report;
	•	consent-compliance review;
	•	investigational-product reconciliation report;
	•	vendor quality report;
	•	audit report;
	•	serious noncompliance or serious breach report;
	•	root-cause analysis;
	•	CAPA plan and effectiveness check;
	•	suspension or termination report.

These documents describe evidence, impact, immediate containment, root cause, corrective action, preventive action, owner, due date and verification of effectiveness.

C8. Data-review and cleaning reports

Data management periodically produces:
	•	enrollment and visit-status report;
	•	missing-form and missing-data report;
	•	outstanding-query report;
	•	external-data reconciliation report;
	•	coding status;
	•	SAE reconciliation status;
	•	laboratory outlier listing;
	•	dosing inconsistency listing;
	•	PK timing deviation listing;
	•	RECIST inconsistency listing;
	•	protocol-deviation listing;
	•	readiness-for-freeze report.

The PI responds to medical or source-dependent queries; the sponsor must not direct the investigator to change a medically valid assessment merely to make the data more convenient.

C9. Registry updates and regulatory correspondence

The responsible party maintains the trial registry record and documents changes to status, enrollment, sites, contacts, outcomes and completion dates. Regulatory affairs also creates:
	•	information amendments;
	•	responses to information requests;
	•	clinical hold responses;
	•	ethics-committee annual progress reports;
	•	investigator notifications;
	•	updated country submissions;
	•	meeting requests and briefing packages.

D. Large documents created after the Phase I trial

D1. Data-cleaning, freeze and database-lock package

Exact sequence
	1.	Last participant reaches the relevant data cutoff.
	2.	Sites enter outstanding data.
	3.	Vendor transfers are finalized.
	4.	Coding is completed.
	5.	SAE, death, exposure, laboratory, imaging and PK data are reconciled.
	6.	Protocol deviations are classified.
	7.	Queries are closed or documented as unresolvable.
	8.	Investigator review and eCRF endorsement are completed.
	9.	Data management circulates a lock-readiness checklist.
	10.	Clinical, safety, statistics and data management approve lock.
	11.	Access is restricted and the database is locked.
	12.	Any post-lock change requires authorization, reason, audit trail, impact assessment and possibly re-lock.

The lock package commonly includes the final Data Management Report, reconciliation certificates, query metrics, coding dictionary versions, transfer status, investigator-signature status, protocol-deviation status, approval checklist and lock memorandum.

D2. Submission datasets and metadata

Data are transformed into:
	•	SDTM domains;
	•	controlled terminology;
	•	supplemental qualifiers where justified;
	•	trial-design domains;
	•	ADaM analysis datasets;
	•	analysis results metadata where used;
	•	Define-XML;
	•	annotated CRF;
	•	data reviewer’s guide;
	•	analysis data reviewer’s guide;
	•	validation reports;
	•	dataset programs and logs;
	•	output programs and logs.

SDTM organizes collected clinical information, ADaM supports analysis and traceability, and Define-XML communicates dataset and variable metadata [13][14][15]. FDA identifies SDTM, ADaM and Define-XML among its supported submission standards, subject to the applicable Data Standards Catalog and study-start rules [23].  ￼

D3. Final statistical outputs and analysis report

After lock:
	1.	the statistician confirms analysis populations;
	2.	deviations from the SAP are documented;
	3.	validated programs generate TFLs;
	4.	independent programming or other validated QC is performed;
	5.	clinical and statistical reviewers inspect outputs;
	6.	the output package is approved for the CSR.

A separate statistical report may be created, but ICH E3 expects the CSR itself to integrate clinical and statistical presentation rather than merely attach two unrelated reports [21].  ￼

D4. Clinical Study Report

CSR source package

The writer receives:
	•	approved protocol and amendments;
	•	final SAP and amendments;
	•	final datasets and TFLs;
	•	disposition and deviation listings;
	•	exposure and dose-modification outputs;
	•	safety outputs;
	•	PK/PD and biomarker reports;
	•	efficacy/activity outputs;
	•	narratives;
	•	quality and monitoring summaries;
	•	committee decisions;
	•	investigator and site information;
	•	sample CRF;
	•	disclosure and appendix requirements.

CSR authoring sequence
	1.	Prepare the shell early. Reuse approved protocol methods.
	2.	Confirm the analysis cutoff and output package.
	3.	Draft methods before lock where stable.
	4.	Populate disposition, exposure, safety, PK/PD and activity results from validated outputs.
	5.	Write patient narratives.
	6.	Discuss limitations and deviations.
	7.	Integrate clinical and statistical interpretation.
	8.	Conduct functional review.
	9.	Perform independent data and editorial QC.
	10.	Obtain physician, statistician and sponsor approvals.
	11.	Publish the report and appendices.
	12.	Submit, disclose and archive as applicable.

CSR content

An oncology Phase I CSR commonly includes:
	•	synopsis;
	•	ethics and administrative structure;
	•	investigators and study sites;
	•	objectives and endpoints;
	•	design and dose-escalation method;
	•	changes to planned conduct or analysis;
	•	participant disposition;
	•	protocol deviations;
	•	analysis populations;
	•	demographics and baseline disease;
	•	prior therapy;
	•	exposure and dose intensity;
	•	DLTs;
	•	treatment-emergent AEs;
	•	treatment-related AEs;
	•	serious AEs;
	•	deaths;
	•	discontinuations and dose modifications;
	•	AESIs;
	•	laboratory, ECG and vital-sign findings;
	•	PK;
	•	PD and biomarkers;
	•	tumor response, duration and time-to-event data where mature;
	•	dose-exposure-safety/activity relationships;
	•	recommended dose rationale;
	•	quality-management issues that could affect reliability;
	•	conclusions;
	•	appendices.

ICH E3 describes the CSR as a single integrated clinical and statistical report containing or appending the protocol, sample CRFs, investigator information, investigational-product information, technical statistical documentation, patient listings and statistical details [21].  ￼

D5. Patient narratives

Narratives are usually prepared for:
	•	deaths;
	•	SAEs;
	•	discontinuations due to AEs;
	•	DLTs;
	•	pregnancies;
	•	AESIs;
	•	other clinically important events defined in the SAP or narrative plan.

The writer assembles a chronological account from exposure, medical history, concomitant medication, AE, laboratory and treatment data. The narrative states relevant diagnostic workup, intervention, dechallenge/rechallenge, outcome and investigator/sponsor causality without obscuring differing assessments.

Narratives undergo:
	•	programmatic data-population QC;
	•	patient-level source/listing reconciliation;
	•	medical review;
	•	consistency review against safety tables and case reports;
	•	privacy and redaction QC.

D6. PK, biomarker, imaging and specialty reports

Separate final reports may include:
	•	bioanalytical method validation;
	•	PK report;
	•	population PK report;
	•	exposure-response report;
	•	immunogenicity report;
	•	biomarker or translational report;
	•	genomic analysis report;
	•	central imaging review report;
	•	pathology review report;
	•	sample-accountability report.

Their final conclusions are summarized in the CSR, while full reports may be submitted as appendices or separate technical reports.

D7. Dose-recommendation and End-of-Phase I package

For the next development decision, the clinical pharmacology and clinical teams integrate:
	•	cumulative safety and tolerability;
	•	DLTs;
	•	chronic or delayed toxicity;
	•	dose intensity and adherence;
	•	PK exposure and variability;
	•	target engagement and PD;
	•	exposure-response;
	•	preliminary activity;
	•	subgroup and biomarker findings;
	•	formulation and food effects;
	•	combination feasibility;
	•	uncertainty and additional data needs.

The resulting dose-optimization report or health-authority briefing package justifies the proposed dose or doses for subsequent development [9].

D8. Registry results and public disclosure

For applicable U.S. trials, the standard ClinicalTrials.gov results deadline is generally no later than one year after the primary completion date, although qualifying certifications, delayed submission and extensions can apply [24]. The submission includes participant flow, baseline characteristics, outcome results, adverse-event information and required administrative data. Registry results are reconciled against the protocol, SAP, CSR and publications before submission.  ￼

Other disclosure outputs can include:
	•	protocol and SAP posting;
	•	redacted CSR;
	•	results synopsis;
	•	lay summary;
	•	EU or country-specific results submissions;
	•	data-sharing package;
	•	anonymization report.

D9. Manuscripts, abstracts and presentations

The publication team develops a publication plan and authorship strategy. Each publication is created from the locked analysis or a prespecified later cutoff:
	1.	define research question and output subset;
	2.	prepare publication-specific shells;
	3.	draft methods consistently with protocol and SAP;
	4.	populate validated results;
	5.	interpret without overstating exploratory activity;
	6.	document author contributions and conflicts;
	7.	perform data verification and disclosure review;
	8.	obtain author approval;
	9.	retain journal correspondence and final versions.

Publication analyses that differ from the SAP are clearly described as post hoc or exploratory.

D10. Trial-closeout and archiving documents

Major closeout documents include:
	•	final investigator report;
	•	site closeout report;
	•	final drug-accountability and destruction certificate;
	•	final laboratory/sample disposition report;
	•	final monitoring report;
	•	outstanding-action closure report;
	•	financial-disclosure updates;
	•	final TMF reconciliation report;
	•	TMF completeness and quality certificate;
	•	archival plan;
	•	retention schedule;
	•	system decommissioning or data-migration plan;
	•	transfer-of-obligations closeout;
	•	final trial summary.

The sponsor and investigator retain records in a form that remains identifiable, version-controlled, searchable, retrievable and traceable [1][25]. FDA’s specific investigator retention rule is at least two years after approval for the studied indication or, if no application is filed or approved, two years after discontinuation and FDA notification; longer sponsor, contractual, product-development or regional periods may apply [3].  ￼

D11. Regulatory submission assembly

Approved reports, datasets and appendices are placed into the applicable eCTD sequence. Publishing performs:
	•	document granularity assessment;
	•	PDF rendering;
	•	bookmarks and hyperlinks;
	•	metadata;
	•	lifecycle operations;
	•	study tagging;
	•	dataset placement;
	•	technical validation;
	•	virus and file-integrity checks;
	•	sequence-level QC;
	•	gateway transmission and receipt reconciliation.

eCTD is FDA’s standard format for applications, amendments, supplements and reports to CDER and CBER; FDA has supported eCTD v4.0 for new applications since September 16, 2024, subject to current implementation restrictions [22].  ￼

Condensed lifecycle dependency map

Nonclinical/CMC evidence
        ↓
Development plan and dose rationale
        ↓
Investigator’s Brochure + protocol + ICF
        ↓
CRF/DMP + SAP/TFL shells + safety/monitoring/manuals
        ↓
IND/CTA and IRB/IEC approval
        ↓
Source data and external vendor data
        ↓
EDC cleaning, coding, safety reconciliation and dose-review packages
        ↓
Amendments, expedited safety reports, DSUR/annual reports
        ↓
Last participant / defined data cutoff
        ↓
Final reconciliation → investigator sign-off → database lock
        ↓
SDTM → ADaM → validated tables, figures and listings
        ↓
CSR + narratives + specialty reports
        ↓
Regulatory meeting/submission + registry disclosure + publications
        ↓
TMF/ISF reconciliation, archive and continuing retention

The central traceability chain is:

Participant source record
→ collected CRF/vendor variable
→ SDTM representation
→ ADaM derivation
→ validated table/figure/listing
→ document statement and conclusion
→ development or regulatory decision

A well-controlled Phase I program should be able to navigate this chain in both directions.
