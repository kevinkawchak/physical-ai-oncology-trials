# Sponsor Organization for Oncology Clinical Trials in 2026
## Chunk 3: Functional Responsibilities — Discovery, IND-Enabling, and Protocol Design

This section is organized by sponsor functions. For each function, the analysis describes how sponsor organizations typically (1) initiate, (2) operationalize and run, (3) manage common/emergent issues, and (4) finish trials in an approval-focused oncology development program. The descriptions reflect 2026 regulatory expectations for oversight, risk management, electronic systems, and transparency. [40]

---

### Discovery and IND-Enabling

**Initiate:** Sponsor research leadership (discovery biology, translational, nonclinical, CMC) aligns on a target/asset hypothesis and a clinical entry strategy. IND-enabling work is organized around nonclinical safety expectations (ICH M3(R2)) and oncology-specific nonclinical principles (ICH S9 for anticancer pharmaceuticals), which shape both the evidence package and timeline gating. [41]

**Operationalize/run:** Sponsors typically stand up an "IND-enabling core team" with RACI that covers toxicology, safety pharmacology, DMPK, bioanalytical, CMC, and regulatory writing. The sponsor must also prepare an Investigator's Brochure (IB) as part of IND content expectations (21 CFR 312.23). [42]

**Handle issues:** Common emergent issues include unexpected toxicology signals, CMC comparability changes, and biomarker assay readiness. Organizationally, this drives early quality involvement (GLP/GMP audits, vendor qualification) and change-control governance because later clinical protocol amendments and dose changes are often traceable to preclinical and manufacturing changes. [43]

**Finish:** "IND-ready" is a decision gate with explicit deliverables: final protocols/synopsis, IB, nonclinical summaries, CMC description, and safety monitoring plan. The sponsor maintains an effective IND and monitoring. [44]

### Protocol Design and Scientific Leadership

**Initiate:** Clinical development and statistics initiate trials by defining indication strategy, endpoints, estimands, dose strategy, and operational feasibility. In oncology, FDA's finalized Project Optimus dose optimization guidance strongly pulls dose strategy decisions earlier and increases the importance of cross-functional alignment among clinical pharmacology, safety, statistics, and clinical science. [45]

**Operationalize/run:** Modern protocol development is increasingly "industrialized" through structured content and controlled change pathways—consistent with ICH M11's electronic structured protocol standardization direction. [17] Protocols often incorporate advanced designs: adaptive features (per FDA adaptive designs guidance), and master protocol structures (per FDA master protocol guidance) to accelerate oncology development where multiple sub-studies share infrastructure. [46]

**Handle issues:**

Protocol amendments: Sponsors manage amendment governance as a portfolio risk: each amendment triggers downstream impacts on contracts, training, monitoring plans, EDC builds, statistical analysis plans, and submissions packages. FDA's RBM and electronic systems guidances effectively require that monitoring plans and electronic system controls keep up with protocol changes. [47]

Enrollment shortfalls: Protocol design now often includes pragmatic burden-reduction tactics and broader eligibility; FDA's 2025 guidance on enhancing participation in trials recommends broadening eligibility criteria and reducing burdens, which sponsors translate into protocol visit schedules and decentralized elements. [48]

Adaptive decision points: Additional governance is needed to preserve trial integrity (access control to interim results, documented decision-making, simulation artifacts). [49]

**Finish:** Protocol completion is not the finish; it is the "design freeze" that enables downstream eTMF filing, system builds, regulatory submissions, and monitoring plan finalization. ICH E6(R3) emphasizes that oversight measures must be fit-for-purpose and tailored, and it explicitly links acceptable ranges (QTLs) to control risks to critical-to-quality factors. [50]
