# Oncology Trial Laws for Patient Control in the AI Era

## Metrics for a patient-control baseline

A future-proof statute should require a public patient-control dashboard. The first domain is **access**: trial-offer rate, median days from pathology or genomic result to first trial shortlist, percentage of patients receiving a machine-readable eligibility report, percentage with same-day or near-real-time release of pathology and genomic outputs to an app of choice, and travel miles or work hours avoided through decentralized participation.[8][10][11][12][16][17][18][21] The second domain is **decision quality**: informed-consent comprehension, decisional-conflict scores, concordance between stated patient goals and selected trial, rate of successful appeals of AI exclusion, and percentage of AI recommendations that include explanation, provenance, and model-version disclosure.[4][5][9][13] The third domain is **control-in-action**: hospitalization risk, urgent-care and ED use, alert-to-clinician response time, false-alert burden, and serious adverse events during home-based or robotics-enabled services.[20][22][26][27]

The fourth domain is **fairness and technical quality**. Any AI system that influences eligibility, treatment timing, dose modification, routing, or remote escalation should have a declared context of use, external validation, subgroup performance reporting, calibration monitoring, update-control disclosure, and documented revalidation after major workflow or model changes.[9][13][17][18][21] Those requirements are not speculative; they are directly aligned with the FDA's credibility-based approach to AI in regulated product development and with HTI-1's movement toward structured source attributes, fairness information, external validation disclosures, and update schedules.[9][13]

A reasonable legislative baseline would therefore be to require structured public reporting on at least three time horizons: near-term access metrics within the first year of implementation, safety and quality metrics within two to three years, and longitudinal equity and retention metrics thereafter.[8][20][23][24][25] That staged approach is important because AI and robotics are improving quickly, but the patient's rights position should improve even if the technology itself changes category from rules engine to foundation model to agentic system.[9][18][21][26][27]

## Implementation guardrails

The most important guardrail is that no patient should be denied trial consideration, continued participation, or needed supportive care solely by an automated output.[9][13][19] That principle follows from three facts: the strongest AI evidence today is still task-specific rather than universally outcome-transforming, at least one randomized oncology deployment failed to improve overall enrollment, and current federal AI policy emphasizes transparency, validity, fairness, and governance rather than blind automation.[9][13][19]

The second guardrail is capability neutrality. If a future system is a retrieval-augmented LLM, a locally hosted oncology language model, a pathology foundation model, a wearable-risk engine, a robotic bronchoscopy platform, or a home-treatment orchestration stack, the same statutory rights should attach whenever the system can materially shape the patient's oncology options, data flow, or bodily intervention.[16][17][18][21][22][26][27] The point is not to predict the winning architecture; it is to lock in patient authority regardless of architecture.[9][13]

The third guardrail is public evaluation. The FDA's April 2026 move toward real-time clinical-trial proof-of-concept programs, along with its continuing DHT program, suggests that trial oversight itself is becoming more continuous and data-rich.[23][24] That makes this an unusually timely moment to require annual public reporting, auditability, and sunset review for any oncology-trial law that authorizes substantial AI or robotics use.[23][24]

The overall conclusion is straightforward. The best near-term oncology-trial laws are not laws that simply "allow more AI." They are laws that give patients durable authority over AI-enabled trial pathways through data portability, reimbursement parity, biomarker access, dynamic consent, explanation, appeal, and validated remote participation.[1][2][5][7][9][10][13][14][21][23][24][25][26][27] If that baseline is established now, future faster multimodal AI and more capable clinical robotics are more likely to expand patient control over cancer rather than relocate control to sponsors, vendors, or institutions.[9][13][18][21][26][27]

## Open questions and limitations

The evidence base is strongest today for AI-assisted matching, remote symptom monitoring, and computational pathology, and weaker for broad claims about autonomous oncology robotics.[16][17][18][20][21][26][27] The legal analysis above is therefore highest-confidence when it recommends rights and governance structures that are technology-neutral, and lower-confidence when it assumes specific future robotics modalities will become routine across oncology trials.[9][13][26][27] There is also an unresolved implementation question about how far state legislation can go without a federal companion framework for uniform patient rights, especially around interoperability, contestability, and decentralized reimbursement.[1][7][10][14]
