# Oncology Trial Laws for Patient Control in the AI Era

## AI and robotics most likely to expand patient control

Among AI tools, retrieval-augmented large language models for trial matching are the clearest near-term control multipliers because they reduce search friction at the exact point where patients lose options. TrialGPT recalled more than 90% of relevant trials while searching less than 6% of the initial collection, achieved 87.3% criterion-level accuracy, improved ranking and exclusion performance by 43.8% over the best comparator, and reduced screening time by 42.6% in a user study.[16] Real-world extensions are moving from prototype to deployable workflow: PRISM validated end-to-end matching on real-world EHRs and reported that a locally hostable oncology model reached 63% question-level accuracy versus 53% for GPT-3.5, while ranking ground-truth trials in the top three 65.3% of the time with an NDCG of 0.68.[17] TrialMatchAI, an early 2026 system, reported that 92% of oncology patients had at least one relevant trial retrieved within the top 20 recommendations and that expert assessment validated over 90% criterion-level eligibility accuracy.[18] For legislation, these are the systems that justify enforceable rights to machine-readable matching, patient-facing plain-language eligibility explanation, and human review before exclusion.[16][17][18]

AI does not, however, improve control automatically. In a prospective randomized trial at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, AI-triggered trial notifications to physicians did not significantly increase overall trial consent or enrollment, even though the workflow was aimed at patients made "trial ready" by AI-detected progression and elevated probability of treatment change.[19] That negative finding is valuable for lawmakers because it shows that trial autonomy depends on the whole pathway from identification to referral, navigation, and patient decision support, not merely on generating a shortlist.[19]

The next high-value layer is predictive monitoring that helps patients act sooner between visits. In a large nonrandomized trial of electronic patient-reported outcome–based remote symptom monitoring, hospitalization risk fell 19% at three months and 13% at six months, with similar patterns across subgroup analyses.[20] AI can sharpen that control function when it is explainable and multimodal: a prospective non–small cell lung cancer study from Moffitt Cancer Center found that Bayesian-network models using demographic, clinical, patient-reported, and wearable data improved urgent-care prediction from AUC 0.72 before systemic therapy and 0.81 during therapy to a final AUC of 0.86 after adding patient-reported outcomes and wearable sensor data.[22] This class of AI does not "decide treatment" directly, but it gives the patient greater operational control over toxicity management, emergency avoidance, and the ability to stay on trial safely.[20][22][24][25]

Computer-vision pathology models may be even more important for control over *which* cancer is being treated and *which* trials are still open to the patient. The Virchow foundation model, trained on approximately 1.5 million whole-slide images from about 100,000 patients at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, achieved an overall pan-cancer AUC of 0.950, an AUC of 0.937 on rare cancers, and 72.5% specificity at 95% sensitivity, while outperforming baseline foundation models on seven of nine biomarker tasks and reducing reliance on some additional tests.[21] In practical terms, that means faster biomarker clarification, earlier confirmation of actionable biology, fewer repeat diagnostic delays, and a larger window for the patient to choose among targeted and biomarker-defined trials.[21]

Robotics matter more selectively, but where they matter they change geography and timing, which are core components of patient control. In the prospective TARGET study of robotic-assisted bronchoscopy, strict adjudicated diagnostic yield was 61.6% and reported complication rates were low at 3.8%.[26] In a separate home-treatment model from Mayo Clinic, Cancer CARE Beyond Walls delivered 93 IV chemotherapy infusions to 10 patients in their homes with no treatment-related infusion reactions or catheter-related infections.[27] The legislative implication is that robotics should not be treated mainly as "autonomy replacement"; in oncology trials, their most defensible near-term value is expanding diagnostic access, reducing travel burden, and moving protocol-compatible treatment closer to where people live.[26][27]

## Ranked document types and bill summaries

The ranking below orders document types by how directly they can increase patient control in oncology trials over the next five to seven years.[1][7][9][10][13]

- **Federal authorizing statute amending the Public Health Service Act and Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.** This is the highest-value vehicle because it can create a national oncology-trial patient rights framework that is not dependent on vendor policy or site culture.[1][3][4][5][7][9] The summary objective should be to establish rights to machine-readable trial matching, dynamic e-consent, AI notice and explanation, documented human review before exclusion, remote-participation election when scientifically appropriate, and rapid release of eligibility-critical pathology and genomic data.[3][5][7][9][10][13][16][17][18]

- **State insurance code amendment.** This is the most important state-level bill type because current coverage rules do not comprehensively protect decentralized participation costs.[1][2][7][8] The summary objective should be routine-cost parity **plus** parity for decentralized oncology-trial components, including telehealth, local labs, mobile phlebotomy, home nursing, remote monitoring devices, and travel or lodging support when hybrid participation is still required.[1][2][7][20][27]

- **Federal health-data rights amendment linked to Cures/HITECH/HIPAA authorities.** This bill type should convert general record-access rights into trial-specific control rights.[10][14][15] Its summary objective should be real-time API export to the patient's chosen application, delivery of structured pathology and genomic results, access logs, correction workflows, and a contestability process for AI-generated eligibility, toxicity, or routing assessments.[10][11][12][13][14][15][21]

- **State medical-practice, telehealth, and delegation amendment.** The summary objective here should be to let qualified local clinicians and home-based teams complete protocol-defined tasks under standardized supervision so that trial participation is no longer tied to an academic-center ZIP code.[7][8][24][25] Covered functions should include remote consenting, delegated assessments, local imaging and laboratory work, and home-treatment support, while preserving sponsor and principal-investigator accountability.[7][8][26][27]

- **Federal biomarker and biospecimen governance statute.** This is the best vehicle for guaranteeing that molecular and pathology outputs relevant to trial eligibility are portable, timely, and reusable under patient-directed consent settings.[5][6][21] Its summary objective should be granular consent for secondary AI training, clinically relevant recontact pathways, and prospective revocation for future secondary use when feasible, paired with timely return of trial-relevant biomarker outputs.[5][6][21]

- **Appropriations and demonstration-authority bill.** This is the right vehicle for scaling what is currently scattered across pilots and guidance.[8][23][24][25] The summary objective should be dedicated funding for the National Cancer Institute, FDA, and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services to run public evaluations of AI-assisted trial matching, rural trial navigation, home-treatment pathways, digital-endpoint validation, and real-time oncology-trial data flows, with mandatory reporting rather than opaque demonstration culture.[8][23][24][25]

- **Model state act or uniform-law package.** This is the fastest diffusion mechanism for states that want to legislate before Congress acts.[7][10][13][14] Its summary objective should be a reusable framework for decentralized-trial parity, AI notice and appeal, data portability, and biomarker access, using technology-neutral definitions that can still govern multimodal models, agentic systems, and future clinical robotics.[7][9][10][13][14]

These are bill types and legislative summaries rather than bill text. That is deliberate, because the strongest near-term contribution is a rights architecture and performance-accountability structure, not a premature attempt at line-by-line statutory drafting.[9][13][19]
