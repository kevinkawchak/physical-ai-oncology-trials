# Oncology Trial Laws for Patient Control in the AI Era

## Core thesis

The current U.S. oncology-trial framework already contains important but fragmented patient-control elements: non-grandfathered health plans must cover routine patient costs for participation in approved clinical trials, Medicare covers routine costs in qualifying clinical trials, and human-subject protections require legally effective informed consent.[1][2][3][4] At the same time, decentralized-trial guidance, electronic access rights, and emerging AI guidance show that the policy environment is moving toward remotely enabled and data-intensive cancer research, but these authorities still do not create a single, enforceable oncology-trial right to data portability, algorithm explanation, or home-based participation.[7][9][10][13][14]

For legislation, "patient control" should be defined operationally rather than rhetorically. It should mean the patient's practical ability to discover relevant trials, understand the benefits and burdens of enrollment, choose where protocol tasks occur when clinically feasible, direct how oncology data and biospecimens are reused, receive timely warnings about toxicity or progression, and challenge an AI-mediated exclusion or recommendation before it becomes consequential.[3][5][6][7][10][13][15] That framing is especially important because oncology trial participation remains low, around 7% in modern datasets, and much of the information needed for eligibility review still sits in unstructured clinical notes rather than clean structured fields.[17]

## Current legal baseline and what it misses

Current law helps patients in four concrete ways. First, it protects consent quality through the Common Rule, FDA informed-consent guidance, and the more recent emphasis on "key information" and facilitating understanding.[3][4][5] Second, it expressly permits broad consent for certain secondary uses of identifiable biospecimens and data, which matters because future oncology AI systems will increasingly depend on linked molecular and clinical datasets.[6] Third, the Cures/information-blocking framework and HIPAA right-of-access rules give patients a strong baseline claim to see and obtain their records.[10][14][15] Fourth, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration now recognizes decentralized clinical-trial elements and digital-health technologies as legitimate tools for remote data acquisition and trial modernization.[7][8][24][25]

The infrastructure is increasingly real rather than hypothetical. In 2024, app-based access to online medical records rose to 57%, and people managing a recent cancer diagnosis reported especially high portal access rates.[11] In the hospital sector, approximately 9 in 10 hospitals enabled patient electronic access via an API in 2024, and 7 in 10 did so via a standards-based API.[12] The ASTP/ONC HTI-1 framework also begins to normalize algorithm transparency by requiring source attributes, external validation information, fairness-related development disclosures, quantitative performance measures, and update schedules for predictive decision support interventions in certified health IT.[13]

But the missing pieces are now more consequential than the existing protections. Neither routine-cost coverage nor FDA decentralized-trial guidance guarantees payment parity for home nursing, local labs, mobile phlebotomy, remote pathology review, remote monitoring devices, or hybrid travel alternatives when fully remote participation is impossible.[1][2][7][8] FDA guidance itself is nonbinding, so it cannot substitute for a statutory patient entitlement.[7] Likewise, current data-access law gives patients access to health information, but not a trial-specific right to receive machine-readable eligibility computations, model version numbers, subgroup performance results, or a formal appeal when AI says they are ineligible or low priority.[10][13][14][15] That is the core policy gap where new oncology trial legislation can materially expand patient control.[1][7][10][13][14]
