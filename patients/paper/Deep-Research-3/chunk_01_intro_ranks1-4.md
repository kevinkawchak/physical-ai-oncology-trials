# Oncology trial laws, AI incorporation, and patient control over cancer: relevance-ranked baseline

The central finding is that U.S. oncology-trial law has not evolved as a single "AI oncology trial statute." Instead, patient control has improved through a layered legal stack: trial transparency, insurance coverage, Medicaid coverage, patient access to health information, patient-experience evidence, decentralized trial authority, diversity action planning, and software/algorithm transparency. AI entered later as an implementation layer built on those statutes: natural-language processing, large language models, knowledge graphs, predictive decision support, in silico modeling, machine learning toxicity prediction, and trial-matching systems that use the data and access pathways created by earlier laws [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [9] [10] [12] [13].

"Patient control" should be interpreted carefully. The laws generally do not prove that patients biologically control tumor growth; they improve control over cancer-care decisions by increasing a patient's ability to find trials, use their own medical information, avoid insurance barriers, consent to investigational options, receive care closer to home, and have trial populations and endpoints better reflect real patients [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]. The best modern AI metrics for this baseline are not survival endpoints alone, but operational and autonomy metrics: time-to-trial recommendation, sensitivity and precision of trial matching, number of eligible trials surfaced, accuracy of biomarker interpretation, patient access to electronic records, coverage-denial reduction, enrollment/retention by subgroup, travel burden, decisional confidence, and whether AI recommendations remain explainable to clinicians and patients [9] [10] [12] [13].

## Rank 1 — H.R. 34, 21st Century Cures Act, Public Law 114-255

This is the most relevant passed law because it connects modern trial design, real-world evidence, patient access to information, interoperability, information-blocking rules, and clinical decision support software. Key oncology-trial provisions include FDA guidance on novel trial designs, FDA evaluation of real-world evidence for new indications, improved patient access to therapies and information, and health IT rules that support patient access to electronic health information [1].

The exact AI type is not a single mandated model; the law created the conditions for AI use through interoperable electronic health information, non-device clinical decision support categories, and later predictive decision support transparency requirements. In practice, this supports oncology trial matching systems using NLP, LLM extraction, rules engines, FHIR-based data exchange, oncology knowledge graphs, predictive decision support, and real-world evidence analytics [1] [9] [10] [12].

Patient control is determined by whether patients and clinicians can access and exchange records, use those records to find trials, reduce information-blocking barriers, and understand the basis of software recommendations. The most relevant metrics are patient access to electronic health information, number of structured data elements available for matching, time from diagnosis or progression to trial recommendation, explainability of decision support, clinician override and feedback rates, and whether a patient can bring their data to another institution or trial-matching tool [1] [9] [10] [12].

## Rank 2 — H.R. 133, Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, Public Law 116-260, including the CLINICAL TREATMENT Act provisions

This law is highly relevant because it required state Medicaid programs to cover routine patient costs for qualifying clinical trials beginning January 1, 2022. It closed a major gap for lower-income patients with cancer and other serious or life-threatening diseases who previously faced inconsistent Medicaid coverage for routine trial-related care [2] [14].

The law itself does not mandate AI. The relevant AI layer is downstream: AI trial-matching tools can identify eligible trials, but the patient's practical control improves only if Medicaid routine-cost coverage makes the match financially usable [2] [14]. The exact AI types most likely to benefit from this law are clinical trial eligibility NLP, LLM summarization of trial criteria, claims/benefit rule engines, and patient-navigation systems that combine eligibility with insurance feasibility [2] [12] [14].

Patient control is determined by coverage availability, fewer financial denials, greater ability to accept trial participation, and increased enrollment feasibility for Medicaid beneficiaries. Useful metrics are Medicaid-covered routine-cost approvals, denial and appeal rates, trial enrollment by Medicaid status, time from match to consent, missed-trial rates caused by coverage barriers, and enrollment among historically underserved groups [2] [14].

## Rank 3 — H.R. 3590, Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, Public Law 111-148

This law is relevant because PHS Act section 2709 requires non-grandfathered group and individual plans to avoid denying participation in approved clinical trials for cancer or another life-threatening disease, avoid denying or limiting routine patient costs, and avoid discrimination based on trial participation [3]. It created a national private-insurance baseline for oncology trial participation before Medicaid coverage was later expanded [3].

The law itself does not use AI. Its AI relevance is that algorithmic trial matching becomes meaningful only when the patient can afford routine care attached to a trial [3] [12]. The practical AI types are payer-benefit automation, rule-based coverage checks, trial-screening algorithms, and patient-facing search systems that can filter by location, phase, disease, biomarker, and likely coverage status [3] [12].

Patient control is determined by whether private insurance removes the "I found a trial, but cannot pay for routine care" barrier. Useful metrics are coverage approval rates, claims denials for routine trial costs, out-of-pocket cost changes, trial consent after insurance verification, and patient-reported financial toxicity [3].

## Rank 4 — H.R. 2617, Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023, Public Law 117-328, including FDORA and FDA Modernization Act 2.0 provisions

This law is highly relevant to future AI baselines because it contains multiple clinical-study modernization provisions. FDORA provisions require diversity action plans for many clinical studies, call for FDA guidance on decentralized clinical studies, and require reporting on whether studies met demographic enrollment goals [4] [11]. Section 3209 also authorizes certain alternatives to animal testing, including cell-based assays and computer models, supporting in vitro, in silico, in chemico, microphysiological systems, and computer-model approaches before or during clinical development [4] [8].

The exact AI types include computer modeling, in silico modeling, ML-based toxicity prediction, digital-twin or synthetic-control concepts, AI/ML drug-development methods, decentralized-trial analytics, and predictive enrollment tools for diversity action plans [4] [8] [11]. These AI types affect oncology by improving preclinical prioritization, helping design trials, identifying underrepresented populations, and potentially making trial participation less site-dependent [4] [8] [11].

Patient control is determined by whether a patient has more realistic access to trials that reflect their demographics, disease biology, geography, and life constraints. Useful metrics are enrollment goals by age, sex, race, ethnicity, geography, and socioeconomic factors; recruitment and retention rates; distance-to-site; decentralized-visit completion; time-to-opening; and whether trial populations match disease prevalence or incidence [4] [11].
