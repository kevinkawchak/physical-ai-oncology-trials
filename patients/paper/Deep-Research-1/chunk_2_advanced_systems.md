## AI-generated survivorship plans and AI coaching

A more recent and more patient-facing example is the Korean QOL+ intervention, an AI-based survivorship care-plan service for posttreatment breast cancer survivors.[9] The accessible abstract describes the intervention as three linked components: mobile collection of survivor-reported outcomes, automatic generation of a survivorship care plan through a website, and face-to-face review and counseling.[9] This is important because it shifts AI from monitoring toxicity toward organizing the entire follow-up plan around the patient's reported problems.[9] In this study, patient control was determined using changes in patient activation, cancer survivor self-efficacy, patient-provider interaction, and quality of life from baseline to one week after the intervention.[9] The pilot reported promising feasibility metrics—participation 70.7%, retention 89.5%, app access 88.2%, task completion 91.2%, and satisfaction 88.2%—and preliminary gains in patient activation (p=0.051, effect size 0.29), self-efficacy (p=0.040, effect size 0.31), and quality of life (p=0.010, effect size 0.42), with no significant change in patient-provider interaction (p=0.098).[9] [[12]](https://suppr.wilddata.cn/browses/detail/pubmed/40478396?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The exact algorithmic engine inside the QOL+ care-plan service is not fully disclosed in the accessible abstract, so it would be overstated to claim that the intervention definitely used a specific random forest or deep-learning model.[9] What can be said with confidence is that the same research program also published a machine-learning prediction study that trained random forest, support vector machine, extreme gradient boosting, and deep-learning models to predict poor posttreatment quality of life in 1,005 Korean cancer survivors.[10] In that companion study, the random forest model performed best, with accuracy 0.85 and F1 score 0.90, and SHAP analysis identified distress, pain, and fatigue as the most important features driving poor quality of life.[10] For present purposes, the important point is that the field is moving from "report symptoms and get advice" to "predict who is likely to lose control over survivorship quality of life and generate a tailored plan before the decline becomes entrenched."[9][10] [[13]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00520-024-08347-z)

Another late-generation example comes from the PATH trial, which tested two AI coaching systems in cancer survivors: MyCoach, a voice-assisted AI delivered through a smart speaker, and SmartText, an autonomous AI delivered through text messages.[11] The exact AI type is unusually well described here. SmartText used a supervised goal-based model, while MyCoach used an unsupervised goal-based model with reward conditions, and both were linked to wearable-sensor feedback.[11] Here, patient control was not measured with a self-efficacy scale. Instead, it was operationalized behaviorally through objectively captured daily steps, wear-time validation, and the proportion of person-days with at least 10,000 steps.[11] MyCoach increased steps by an average of 3618.2 per day over follow-up, with a net gain of 3568.9 steps per day versus control and 2160.6 versus SmartText; 61% of person-days in the MyCoach arm reached at least 10,000 steps compared with 28% in control.[11] [[14]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00539-9)

This trial matters because it shows that newer AI can improve actual execution of a health behavior closely tied to cancer survivorship, recurrence risk, and cardiometabolic risk, even when it does not directly measure subjective control.[11] The increase in control here is therefore operational rather than attitudinal: patients became more able to carry out a protective behavior consistently.[11] That is a meaningful form of control, but it is not the same as feeling more confident in decision-making or symptom management. Future baseline comparisons should keep those forms of control separate.[11] [[14]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00539-9)

## Baseline for future AI and robotics

Taken together, the evidence supports a clear historical progression.[1][2][4][6][9][11] The late-2000s baseline was mainly rule-based toxicity management. The next stage added EHR integration and validated self-efficacy outcomes. The newest stage combines multimodal data, predictive models, automated survivorship planning, and autonomous coaching.[2][4][5][6][9][10][11] Across that arc, patients gained more control in three robust ways: faster recognition and escalation of symptoms, higher confidence in managing treatment effects, and more personalized guidance for survivorship behaviors and follow-up planning.[3][4][5][9][11] [[15]](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53834/)

The practical baseline for future comparison is therefore not "Does the system use AI?" but "How much patient control does it actually add, and on what kind of control?"[3][5][6][9][11] Current oncology AI trials set the baseline roughly at these levels: meaningful gains in self-efficacy are possible but usually modest; patient activation often starts high and may be harder to shift; remote monitoring reliably improves reassurance and timeliness; personalized survivorship planning can improve self-efficacy and quality of life in small pilot settings; and autonomous coaching can produce measurable behavior change when tightly coupled to wearables and interactive feedback.[3][5][7][9][11] [[16]](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53834/)

For future faster AI and robotics, that means the comparison standard should be explicit. A next-generation system should outperform today's software-only baseline on validated agency measures such as CASE-cancer, PAM, or disease-management self-efficacy; on objective management metrics such as symptom-burden reduction, adherence, or behavior execution; and, ideally, on more disease-relevant endpoints such as treatment continuity, preventable emergency use, or longer-term survivorship quality of life.[4][5][6][9][11] Just as importantly, the present literature shows that current evidence is overwhelmingly about software, sensing, and decision support, not robotics.[6][9][11] So any future claim that robotics gives patients greater control should be judged against this software baseline rather than assumed in advance.[6][9][11] [[17]](https://www.journalslibrary.nihr.ac.uk/pgfar/FDDE8516)

## Open questions and limitations

The main limitation of the current evidence base is that direct measurement of "sense of control" is still uncommon.[5][6][8][9][11] Most trials measure adjacent constructs—self-efficacy, activation, distress, quality of life, or behavior change—rather than a global, cancer-specific control construct.[5][6][8][9][11] A second limitation is that some of the most technically advanced systems remain pilots, survivorship studies, or mixed-evidence studies rather than large, definitive trials in patients actively navigating complex treatment decisions.[6][7][8][9] A third limitation is that public summaries do not always disclose the full algorithmic stack, which makes exact comparisons between rule-based systems, machine-learning systems, and hybrid models more difficult than it should be.[8][9][10] [[18]](https://www.journalslibrary.nihr.ac.uk/pgfar/FDDE8516)

Even with those limitations, the baseline is already strong enough to support a careful conclusion: as oncology AI has improved from rule-based alerting to predictive, multimodal, and behavior-shaping systems, patients in trials have generally gained more day-to-day control over symptom response, self-management, and survivorship planning, although the largest and most reproducible gains are still in supportive care and behavior execution rather than in direct treatment-choice autonomy.[3][4][5][6][9][11] That is the most defensible baseline against which future powerful AI and robotics should be judged.[3][4][5][6][9][11] [[19]](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53834/)
