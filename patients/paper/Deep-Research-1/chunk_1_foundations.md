# AI and Patient Control in Oncology Trials

## What the evidence means by patient control

The clearest answer from the oncology trial literature is that patients have, in fact, gained more practical control over their cancer experience as AI-related systems have become more numerous and more capable, but that gain has usually been measured indirectly rather than with one single "sense of control" instrument.[2][5][6][11] In the earlier studies, control was mostly operationalized as better symptom surveillance, quicker escalation, and lower toxicity burden; in the middle generation, it was measured more explicitly through self-efficacy and health-status instruments; and in the newest generation, it is increasingly assessed through patient activation, personalized survivorship planning, multimodal monitoring, and behavior change metrics such as adherence or daily step counts.[2][5][6][9][11] [[1]](https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/131094863/The_eSmart.pdf)

A useful baseline definition is therefore this: in oncology AI trials, "patient control" has usually meant one or more of four things—informational control over what is happening, behavioral control over what the patient can do next, relational control over when the care team responds, and decisional confidence about how to manage symptoms or survivorship.[3][4][5][6][9] Direct measures of that control were strongest when trials used validated scales such as the Communication and Attitudinal Self-Efficacy scale for cancer, the Patient Activation Measure, or self-efficacy scales for chronic disease management; where those were absent, the studies relied on good but still indirect proxies such as symptom burden, fatigue, anxiety, distress, quality of life, or physical activity.[3][4][5][6][9][11] [[2]](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53834/)

## Early baseline from rule-based symptom AI

The first convincing baseline for AI-like patient control in oncology came from the Advanced Symptom Management System, or ASyMS, which was not "AI" in the modern large-model sense but did combine mobile symptom reporting, rule-based clinical decision support, CTCAE-linked symptom logic, automated self-care advice, and clinician alerts.[1][2][3] In the original trial era, the system evaluated patient control primarily as lower incidence, severity, and distress of chemotherapy toxicities such as nausea, vomiting, fatigue, mucositis, hand-foot syndrome, and diarrhea, meaning that control was still understood mainly as toxicity containment rather than as patient agency in a psychological sense.[1] Later eSMART protocol work made the patient-agency goal more explicit by planning to assess symptom burden, quality of life, supportive care needs, anxiety, and self-care self-efficacy alongside the remote monitoring itself.[2] [[3]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23416817_Evaluation_of_a_mobile_phone-based_advanced_symptom_management_system_ASyMSC_in_the_management_of_chemotherapy-related_toxicity?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The exact AI type here was a low-to-moderate intelligence architecture: deterministic symptom algorithms, predictive risk modeling, and traffic-light escalation rules rather than machine learning classifiers trained on large datasets.[2][3] That distinction matters for a future baseline. These systems already gave patients more control by letting them report symptoms from home, receive immediate self-care guidance, view symptom trends, and trigger clinician review before a problem became a crisis.[2][3] Put differently, the earliest generation increased control by shrinking the gap between symptom onset and action.[2][3] [[4]](https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/131094863/The_eSmart.pdf)

The later qualitative evaluation of ASyMS after its deployment in the multicountry eSMART trial shows what this felt like to patients. The dominant patient themes were reassurance, enhanced communication and relationships, knowing what was "normal" and what to expect, and an overall enhancement of the cancer care experience.[3] That same paper also reports that the underlying randomized trial found significant and sustained reductions in symptom burden during chemotherapy together with improvements in anxiety, quality of life, self-efficacy, and unmet supportive care needs.[3] This is one of the clearest examples in the literature where increasing technical capability translated into a stronger subjective sense that the illness was being watched, interpreted, and acted on with the patient rather than after the patient had already lost control.[2][3] [[5]](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53834/)

## EHR-integrated adverse-event AI and the move toward explicit self-efficacy

The phase III eRAPID program marked a higher-quality stage because it was not only an online symptom-reporting tool but an EHR-integrated adverse-event system that generated severity-dependent self-management advice, prompted hospital contact for serious symptoms, and made patient reports visible inside routine clinical workflows.[4][5] Technically, eRAPID was again closer to advanced rule-based clinical AI than to modern machine learning: it used structured patient-reported adverse events, automated severity-based advice, clinician notifications for severe events, and real-time integration into the clinical record.[4][5] The important shift was that this generation measured control more explicitly.[4][5] [[6]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12885-017-3303-8)

In eRAPID, patient control over health was determined through a combination of symptom-related quality-of-life measures, perceived overall health, and direct self-efficacy assessment.[4][5] The trial's primary endpoint was the FACT-G Physical Well-Being score at 18 weeks, and its secondary control-relevant outcomes included a self-efficacy scale, EQ-5D measures, hospital contacts, and costs.[4][5] The most important result for the present question is that self-efficacy improved significantly at 18 weeks in the intervention arm, with a mean advantage of 0.48 points over usual care, a 95% confidence interval of 0.13 to 0.83, and p=0.0073.[5] Patients also reported better overall health on the EQ-5D visual analogue scale at 12 and 18 weeks, and the program found earlier gains in symptom control at 6 and 12 weeks even though the primary 18-week symptom endpoint was not met.[4][5] [[7]](https://www.journalslibrary.nihr.ac.uk/pgfar/FDDE8516)

This makes eRAPID especially valuable as a baseline comparator. It shows that once oncology digital systems moved from standalone alerting into a shared patient-clinician record, the measurable benefit shifted from mere symptom capture to explicit patient confidence in managing treatment side effects.[4][5] In other words, the middle-generation systems did not just tell the patient what to do; they measurably improved the patient's confidence that they could do it.[4][5] That is a more mature form of control than the earlier ASyMS baseline, even though the underlying AI was still mostly rules and thresholds rather than black-box machine learning.[4][5] [[8]](https://www.journalslibrary.nihr.ac.uk/pgfar/FDDE8516)

## Predictive and multimodal survivorship AI

The PERSIST program represents a newer stage in which oncology AI moved beyond symptom questionnaires into multimodal sensing, predictive modeling, and higher-level decision support for survivorship.[6][7][8] Its protocol describes a mobile health app supported by a data-driven clinical decision support system, predictive models trained on big health data, smart-band data capture, software sensing for mood estimation and depressive symptom recognition, and a Multimodal Risk Assessment and Symptom Tracking framework that fused objective markers such as vital signs with subjective markers such as PROMs, PREMs, and linguistic, vocal, and facial cues.[6][8] This is qualitatively more advanced than ASyMS or eRAPID because it adds multimodal inference and big-data prediction to the patient-monitoring loop.[6][8] [[9]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12911-021-01603-w)

PERSIST is also useful because it specified in advance how patient control would be determined.[6] The protocol made increased perceived self-efficacy, measured with CASE-cancer, the primary endpoint and patient activation, measured with PAM, a secondary endpoint.[6] That is a more direct operationalization of control than earlier toxicity-centric endpoints.[6] The later study reports that the platform engaged survivors, that clinical trial attendance was high at 75.3%, and that the app improved engagement and usability over time while supporting self-management and personalized care plans.[7][8] At the same time, the accessible manuscript details show a more sobering signal: patients generally started with high CASE-cancer and PAM scores, and no statistically significant changes were found across CASE-cancer factors or PAM levels, even though qualitative feedback suggested that participants felt the monitoring helped them follow their health and "gain control over their condition."[7][8] [[10]](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/73g6arq7/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That mixed pattern is one of the most important baseline findings for future AI and robotics. More sophisticated AI did not automatically produce large, statistically significant gains in perceived control once patients already entered the study with relatively high activation and self-efficacy.[6][7][8] What it did produce was sustained engagement, richer monitoring, personalized care plans, and positive qualitative reports of empowerment.[7][8] For future systems, that means greater algorithmic complexity alone is not enough. To exceed the current baseline, next-generation AI will have to improve the patient's lived sense of control by a margin that is visible on validated agency measures, not just on engineering complexity.[6][7][8] [[11]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12911-021-01603-w)
