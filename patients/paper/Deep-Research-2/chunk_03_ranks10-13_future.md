# Oncology trial laws, AI incorporation, and patient control over cancer: relevance-ranked baseline (continued)

## Rank 10 — FDA Clinical Decision Support Software Guidance implementing 21st Century Cures Act section 3060

This FDA guidance is relevant because section 3060 of the Cures Act amended the device definition to exclude certain software functions, including some decision support software, from FDA device regulation when criteria are met [10]. The guidance clarifies when CDS is non-device CDS and when software remains regulated as a device [10].

The exact AI type includes non-device CDS, device software functions, patient- or caregiver-facing software, and clinician-facing software that analyzes or presents medical information. For oncology trials, this matters when software recommends trial options, summarizes biomarker eligibility, flags contraindications, or prioritizes therapies [10].

Patient control is determined by whether the system provides the basis for recommendations so clinicians do not rely primarily on opaque automation. Useful metrics are explanation completeness, auditability, human-review time, error rates, unsafe recommendation rates, clinician acceptance, patient comprehension, and separation between informational trial matching and directive medical-device outputs [10].

## Rank 11 — FDA Diversity Action Plans draft guidance under FDORA

This is not a bill, but it is central to using FDORA in oncology trials because it specifies the expected form, content, timing, and evaluation of diversity action plans. It is especially relevant for cancer because oncology trials have historically struggled with underrepresentation by race, ethnicity, age, geography, income, language, and access to specialty centers [11].

The exact AI type is not mandated, but sponsors can use predictive analytics, epidemiologic modeling, site-selection algorithms, community-referral analytics, and trial-feasibility models to set and meet enrollment goals. The risk is that biased data or poorly calibrated models could reproduce inequities, so fairness and subgroup performance must be measured [9] [11].

Patient control is determined by whether more patients see trials designed for populations like them and available through sites or decentralized methods they can realistically use. Useful metrics are demographic enrollment goals versus actual enrollment, screening-to-consent by subgroup, retention by subgroup, geographic reach, language access, and reasons for screen failure [11].

## Rank 12 — Neuro-symbolic, multi-agent AI oncology trial matching evidence

This is not a law, but it is the strongest current evidence document for how the legal infrastructure can be converted into patient-level control. A 2026 prospective evaluation of neuro-symbolic, multi-agent AI for oncology trial matching reported an F1 score of 0.82, reduced screening time from about 120 minutes to about 30 minutes per patient, processed more than 157,000 clinical pages, and reported no demographic subgroup F1 gap greater than 10 percentage points [12].

The exact AI type is neuro-symbolic multi-agent AI with LLM-based extraction, ontology-grounded deterministic eligibility reasoning, an oncology knowledge graph, prioritization, and expert-curated corpora [12]. This is more advanced than simple keyword search because it combines statistical language understanding with rule-based eligibility logic and auditability [12].

Patient control is determined by faster and more complete identification of trial options, while preserving clinician review. Useful metrics are sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1, calibration, time-to-recommendation, oncologist-confirmed matches, subgroup performance gaps, and number of trial opportunities found that manual screening would miss [12].

## Rank 13 — LLM biomarker-based oncology trial matching evidence

This is not a law, but it is directly relevant because modern oncology eligibility increasingly depends on biomarkers, variants, tumor type, prior therapy, stage, organ function, and timing. A 2025 study on biomarker-based oncology trial matching using large language models focused on extracting eligibility information scattered across unstructured text and matching genomic biomarkers to precision oncology trials [13].

The exact AI type is large language model–based extraction and matching for biomarker-driven oncology trials. This complements the legal baseline created by ClinicalTrials.gov reporting, Cures Act interoperability, and FDORA diversity/decentralization provisions [1] [4] [5] [13].

Patient control is determined by whether molecular information becomes actionable earlier and more accurately. Useful metrics are biomarker extraction accuracy, variant normalization accuracy, match precision, false exclusion rate, false inclusion rate, time from molecular report to trial option, and patient understanding of why a trial is or is not a fit [13].

# Baseline versus future AI and robotics

The baseline established by these laws is strong on access rights but weaker on automated execution. Patients gained legal control over trial affordability, transparency, data access, informed consent, and participation pathways, but these rights still require clinicians, research coordinators, insurers, and sponsors to act quickly and coherently [1] [2] [3] [5] [6]. AI improves the baseline by compressing manual screening, translating unstructured medical records into eligibility features, reading trial protocols, detecting biomarker matches, and ranking feasible options [12] [13].

The biggest remaining gap is that law often guarantees the right to coverage, information, or a process, while AI determines whether the patient actually sees the right option in time. For future AI and robotics, the baseline should be measured against "patient time-to-control": time from scan or molecular result to understandable treatment options, time from option to consent, time from consent to first trial visit, and time from progression to next valid trial match [1] [2] [3] [4] [12] [13].

More powerful AI could give patients more control if it combines legal entitlements with validated clinical reasoning. The most important future systems will likely combine LLMs, knowledge graphs, EHR interoperability, genomic interpretation, claims/coverage logic, decentralized trial logistics, remote monitoring, digital twins, and robotics for biopsy, imaging, drug delivery, surgery, or home-based sample collection [1] [4] [9] [10] [12] [13].

Future systems should not be judged only by speed. They should be judged by calibrated accuracy, subgroup fairness, explainability, patient comprehension, coverage feasibility, reduction in missed trials, reduced travel burden, improved retention, fewer avoidable adverse events, and whether patients report greater decisional agency over their cancer trajectory [9] [10] [11] [12].
