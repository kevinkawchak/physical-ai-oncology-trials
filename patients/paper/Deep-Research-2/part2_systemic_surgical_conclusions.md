# AI in Oncology Trials and Patient Outcomes (continued)

## How AI has improved systemic therapy selection and dosing

Systemic therapy is where the promise of AI is the most intuitively important to patients, but where the evidence is also more heterogeneous. The earliest widely cited clinical example is a 2018 metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer case in which CURATE.AI prospectively guided dosing of ZEN-3694 plus enzalutamide. The AI type was a small-data, patient-specific dosing platform that models an individual dose-response surface and dynamically updates recommendations using only that patient's own longitudinal data. In later formal descriptions of the same platform, the core model is described as a second-order polynomial or quadratic dose-response relation derived from earlier neural-network work. Safety in the 2018 case was judged by improved treatment tolerance and the patient's ability to remain on the combination regimen; efficacy was judged by sustained control of PSA, reduced lesion size, durable response, and absence of disease progression. The report concluded that AI-guided dose adjustment increased both efficacy and tolerance, but because it was an N-of-1 case it should be interpreted as proof of possibility, not proof of generalizable superiority [3,4].

The most rigorous clinical development of this dosing approach so far is the 2025 PRECISE CURATE.AI feasibility trial in advanced solid tumors treated with capecitabine-based therapy. The AI type was again a patient-specific small-data dosing system using a second-order polynomial regression model built from each patient's own dose-response data. Safety was explicitly built into the protocol: recommendations were constrained to 50%-100% of standard-of-care capecitabine doses, all doses had to remain within a patient-specific clinician-defined safe range, grade 3-4 toxicity triggered downward restriction, and physicians could reject or override recommendations. Efficacy was tracked using tumor-marker trajectories and RECIST monitoring, while implementation efficacy was assessed through on-time recommendation delivery and physician adherence. Among 49 dosing events, CURATE.AI was considered relevant in 36, all 36 recommendations were delivered on time, physicians followed 35 of 36 recommendations, five patients had grade 3 toxicities, none had grade 4 toxicities, and only three patients reached the efficacy-driven dosing stage as responders. This is therefore a genuine patient-safety advance in the sense of controlled personalization under physician supervision, but it remains a feasibility baseline rather than a definitive demonstration that AI dosing improves progression-free or overall survival across populations [4].

The strongest current systemic-therapy evidence is not AI choosing the drug de novo, but AI helping identify which patients should receive intensification and which patients can safely avoid it. In localized prostate cancer, a 2023 AI predictive model for hormone therapy used digitized pretreatment pathology slides plus clinical data to identify who would benefit from adding short-term androgen deprivation therapy to radiotherapy. The AI type was a deep-learning multimodal model built from digital pathology images and clinical variables. Safety was determined indirectly but clinically meaningfully: men classified as unlikely to benefit could potentially avoid ADT's sexual, metabolic, and quality-of-life toxicities. Efficacy was determined by distant metastasis. The model-positive subgroup contained 543 men, or 34% of the cohort, and in that subgroup ADT significantly reduced distant metastasis compared with radiotherapy alone, with a subdistribution hazard ratio of 0.34. In the model-negative subgroup of 1,051 men, or 66% of the cohort, there was no significant benefit, with a subdistribution hazard ratio of 0.92. This is one of the clearest examples of AI simultaneously improving safety and efficacy: it helps intensify treatment where benefit is real and de-intensify where benefit is absent [9].

The same logic was extended in a 2025 phase III-trial-based validation for long-term versus short-term ADT in high-risk localized prostate cancer. The AI type was a multimodal AI-derived predictive biomarker using pretreatment digital biopsy images plus age, PSA, Gleason score, and T stage from six phase III randomized radiotherapy trials. Safety was again framed as avoidance of unnecessary long-term hormone exposure, while efficacy was judged primarily by distant metastasis. The published validation reported that benefit from long-term ADT was confined to biomarker-positive men, while biomarker-negative men did not show meaningful benefit; the estimated 15-year distant-metastasis risk difference between radiotherapy plus long-term ADT and radiotherapy plus short-term ADT was 14% in biomarker-positive men and 0% in biomarker-negative men. That result is clinically important because long-term ADT is burdensome, and an AI test that can identify men with no measurable metastasis benefit changes the safety-efficacy balance at the treatment-decision level [10].

A 2025 conference-stage analysis using the same broader multimodal-digital-pathology strategy pushed this principle into drug selection for abiraterone in high-risk nonmetastatic prostate cancer. The AI type was a validated multimodal pathology-and-clinical-data algorithm applied to archival biopsy slides from more than 1,000 men in the STAMPEDE platform. Safety was determined by identifying men who could avoid a drug associated with additional monitoring for hypertension, liver abnormalities, and slightly increased diabetes and heart-attack risk; efficacy was determined by mortality. In the biomarker-positive subgroup, representing about 25% of the men studied, abiraterone reduced 5-year mortality from 17% to 9%. In biomarker-negative men, 5-year mortality moved from 7% to 4%, which investigators described as not statistically or clinically significant. This is exactly the kind of baseline future AI systems should be judged against: not just whether they predict risk, but whether they produce a treatment-allocation rule that both improves hard outcomes in the right subgroup and reduces unnecessary toxicity exposure in the rest [11,12].

## What current surgical and robotic-adjacent evidence shows

The cleanest bridge between current AI and future oncology robotics is not yet fully autonomous cancer surgery, but AI-assisted intraoperative navigation. A 2025 prospective randomized study in early-stage lung cancer surgery evaluated a non-contact AI-assisted intraoperative 3D navigation system during segmentectomy. The AI type was a gesture-controlled navigation platform using deep-learning hand tracking plus convolutional neural-network gesture recognition, error back-propagation with adaptive learning rates, and support-vector-machine-based risk minimization. Safety was determined through blood loss, surgical success rate, operation time, sterility-preserving non-contact control, and implicitly reduced anesthesia exposure; efficacy was determined through navigation time, successful execution of segmentectomy, and surgeon-rated usability. Compared with traditional 3D navigation, the AI-assisted group had substantially shorter navigation time, 50-107 seconds versus 120-234 seconds, and shorter operation time, 84.23±13.18 minutes versus 101.84±11.01 minutes, with similar blood loss and similar surgical success rates. This is a meaningful safety-and-efficiency baseline for future robotic oncology platforms, but it still stops short of proving superior oncologic control, margin status, recurrence reduction, or survival benefit [13].

## Baseline conclusions for future AI and robotics

The historical pattern is now clear. The earliest oncology AI that affected patient care directly was small-scale and highly supervised, such as individualized dose optimization in a single patient [3,4]. The next phase centered on radiotherapy, where deep learning reduced contouring time and reduced clinically important variation while preserving target coverage [5-8]. The most mature current phase is biomarker-guided treatment selection, where multimodal AI applied to archived phase III trial material is beginning to show that some patients can be spared burdensome therapies while others can be identified for escalation with better metastasis or mortality outcomes [9-12].

That means the fairest present-day baseline is this: current oncology AI is already improving patient safety and efficacy, but mostly by making clinician-supervised decisions more precise rather than by autonomously treating cancer. The most convincing hard-outcome gains today come from AI-guided treatment selection in prostate cancer, where AI identifies who should receive or avoid hormonal intensification or abiraterone [9-12]. The broadest real-world gains come from radiotherapy, where AI reduces variability, increases contour quality, and shortens planning and adaptive-treatment workflows without sacrificing dose coverage [5-8]. The most natural baseline for future robotics comes from AI-assisted surgical navigation, where efficiency and sterility-preserving interaction are already improving, but long-term oncologic endpoints still need validation [13].

If future AI or robotic systems are to claim that they meaningfully exceed the present standard, they should beat today's baseline on the outcomes that matter most to patients: fewer grade 3 or higher toxicities, fewer unnecessary systemic treatments, better patient-reported quality of life, lower local-failure and distant-metastasis rates, better overall survival when treatment is intensified, lower major-edit or override rates when AI is used for planning, and clinically sustainable session or operating times without shifting hidden burden back onto the oncology team [2,4-13].

## Open questions and limitations

The evidence base is advancing, but it still has important limits. Many studies still rely on surrogate endpoints such as contour similarity, dosimetry, or planning time rather than survival or patient-reported toxicity [2,5-8]. Some of the strongest treatment-selection results come from AI applied retrospectively to archived material from randomized phase III trials, which is much stronger than single-center retrospective validation but still not the same as prospectively randomizing patients to AI-guided versus non-AI-guided care [2,9,10]. The abiraterone-selection result is particularly promising, but at the time reflected here it was still conference-stage and publicly summarized through institutional reporting rather than a full peer-reviewed manuscript [11,12]. Finally, many of the safest currently deployed systems remain clinician-in-the-loop systems, meaning today's measured benefit is best understood as augmented oncology rather than autonomous oncology [2,4-13].
