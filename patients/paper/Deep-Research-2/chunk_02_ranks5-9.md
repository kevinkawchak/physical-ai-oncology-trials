# Oncology trial laws, AI incorporation, and patient control over cancer: relevance-ranked baseline (continued)

## Rank 5 — H.R. 3580, Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act of 2007, Public Law 110-85

This law is relevant because FDAAA section 801 made clinical trial registration and results reporting a legal infrastructure for ClinicalTrials.gov. It requires registration of applicable clinical trials and submission of structured results information for many FDA-regulated drug, biological, and device trials [5].

The exact AI type is not in the statute, but FDAAA created the searchable and structured trial corpus that later AI systems use. Modern oncology matching systems use NLP, embeddings, LLM extraction, biomarker normalization, ontology mapping, trial-eligibility parsers, and knowledge-graph reasoning on registry records and protocols [5] [12] [13].

Patient control is determined by public access to trial existence, eligibility criteria, locations, outcomes, and adverse events. Useful metrics are registration completeness, results submission timeliness, number of searchable oncology trials, quality of eligibility fields, match accuracy, number of viable trials surfaced to a patient, and reduced dependence on a single institution's trial menu [5] [12] [13].

## Rank 6 — S. 204, Trickett Wendler, Frank Mongiello, Jordan McLinn, and Matthew Bellina Right to Try Act of 2017, Public Law 115-176

This law is relevant to patient control because it allows eligible terminally ill patients who exhausted approved options and cannot participate in a clinical trial to seek access to certain investigational drugs after completion of a phase 1 trial, subject to physician certification, informed consent, and manufacturer agreement [6].

The law does not incorporate AI. AI can support the process only indirectly by identifying investigational products, checking phase 1 completion, summarizing trial status, and helping patients and physicians compare expanded access, clinical trial, and Right to Try pathways [6].

Patient control is determined by the existence of an additional legal pathway outside a trial, not by guaranteed access or proven efficacy. Useful metrics are number of annual manufacturer reports, time from request to decision, number of patients treated, adverse events, patient understanding through informed consent, and comparison with FDA expanded access outcomes [6].

## Rank 7 — H.R. 2430, FDA Reauthorization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-52

This law is relevant because it renewed user-fee programs and reinforced patient-focused drug development commitments. Its relevance to oncology trials is indirect but important: patient-experience data and patient-focused endpoints can affect how trials are designed, reviewed, and interpreted [7].

The exact AI type is not statutory. The downstream AI types are NLP of patient-experience narratives, ML analysis of patient-reported outcomes, digital symptom monitoring, and decision-support tools that can help align endpoints with what patients value, such as pain, fatigue, function, treatment burden, and quality of life [7].

Patient control is determined by whether patient experience becomes evidence in trial design and regulatory review rather than an afterthought. Useful metrics are inclusion of patient-reported outcomes, endpoint changes based on patient input, label language reflecting patient experience, patient preference studies, and usability of trial information for shared decision-making [7].

## Rank 8 — California SB 37, chaptered 2001, cancer clinical trial routine-cost coverage

California SB 37 is a key state-law precedent because it required many California health plans to cover routine patient care costs for patients diagnosed with cancer and accepted into phase I, II, III, or IV cancer clinical trials, if the treating physician determined that participation had meaningful potential to benefit the patient [8].

The exact AI type is none, because this law predates current AI trial matching. Its relevance to AI is that state-level coverage rules created a practical access environment in which later matching tools could produce actionable trial options rather than unaffordable suggestions [8].

Patient control is determined by whether cancer patients can consider trial participation without losing ordinary coverage for physician visits, hospitalization, lab tests, monitoring, prevention of complications, and care arising from complications. Useful metrics are state-level coverage approvals, patient uptake of trials, routine-cost denials, appeals, and the proportion of AI-surfaced matches that are financially actionable [8].

## Rank 9 — ONC HTI-1 Final Rule implementing 21st Century Cures Act algorithm-transparency provisions

This is not a passed bill, but it is a highly relevant implementing document because it operationalizes Cures Act health IT policy for AI and predictive algorithms. HTI-1 establishes first-of-its-kind transparency requirements for AI and other predictive algorithms that are part of certified health IT and supports access, exchange, and use of electronic health information [9].

The exact AI type is predictive decision support intervention, defined broadly as technology that supports decision-making based on algorithms or models that derive relationships from training data and produce prediction, classification, recommendation, evaluation, or analysis [9]. In oncology, this can include risk stratification, trial-screening recommendations, toxicity risk prediction, recurrence prediction, and referral prioritization [9].

Patient control is determined by whether clinicians and health systems can inspect algorithm information for fairness, appropriateness, validity, effectiveness, and safety. Useful metrics are availability of algorithm documentation, subgroup performance, calibration, bias testing, clinical feedback capture, override rates, and whether patients can understand that AI is influencing options presented to them [9].
