# AI in Oncology Trials and Patient Outcomes

## Scope and baseline

The most defensible baseline for oncology today is that artificial intelligence has already produced measurable patient benefit, but that benefit is unevenly distributed across use cases: the strongest evidence is in radiotherapy planning and treatment selection, while fully autonomous cancer treatment remains rare and true robotics-led outcome gains are still early. A 10-year review of the U.S. clinical-trial registry identified 50 completed oncology AI trials by 2025, about two-thirds of them interventional, with radiology, colonoscopy, pathology, surgery, and radiation oncology among the leading domains. A separate systematic review of prospective postdiagnostic cancer-pathway studies found only 15 eligible studies through May 2023 and concluded that most oncology AI work still remained experimental rather than broadly deployed in routine care [1,2].

For a baseline that can be compared fairly against future, faster AI systems and robotic platforms, it is important to separate **process improvements** from **patient-health improvements**. In the current literature, safety has usually been determined by metrics such as grade 3-4 toxicity rates, organ-at-risk dose exposure, major-edit or override rates, treatment-session feasibility, sterility-preserving workflow, or avoidance of ineffective therapy. Efficacy has usually been determined by metrics such as target-volume coverage, distant metastasis, mortality, prostate-specific antigen or carcinoembryonic antigen response, lesion size, RECIST response, or continued treatment without progression [2-12].

## How AI has improved radiotherapy safety and efficacy

One of the clearest patient-facing AI success stories is radiotherapy contouring and adaptive planning. In 2021, a prospective clinical implementation study of deep-learning contour autosegmentation for MR-based prostate radiotherapy planning evaluated 173 patients. The AI type was a deep-learning autosegmentation system used to generate target and organ-at-risk contours before physician review. Safety was assessed through volumetric Dice-Sørensen coefficient, surface Dice-Sørensen coefficient, added path length, and physician protocol-deviation scoring; efficacy was assessed through whether clinically usable contours could be produced faster. The study found a median contouring time of 28 minutes, a 12-minute savings versus historical controls, and reported that 65% of autosegmented cases required no more than minor edits, although 33% still required major clinically significant edits. That combination is crucial for a baseline: AI materially improved efficiency, but safe use still depended on clinician oversight rather than autonomy [6].

The 2022 multicenter observational validation study in non-small-cell lung cancer moved the evidence base from single-center implementation toward broader clinical generalizability. The AI type was a deep-learning CT segmentation model for primary tumors and involved lymph nodes used in radiotherapy targeting. Safety was determined not only by geometric overlap metrics, but also by functional validation: equivalent radiation-dose coverage compared with expert contours, test-retest stability, failure-mode analysis, and eight-expert end-user testing. Efficacy was determined by maintenance of expert-equivalent target coverage together with workflow acceleration. Across 2,208 patients, the AI-assisted workflow reduced segmentation time by 65% and reduced interobserver variability by 32%, while preserving equivalent radiation dose coverage to expert contours. This matters clinically because interobserver variability in contouring can propagate into target miss or unnecessary normal-tissue irradiation, so reductions in variability are a legitimate safety gain rather than a mere convenience gain [5].

By 2025 and 2026, the quality of evidence improved further through prospective multicenter studies and AI-integrated adaptive platforms. In a 2026 prospective multicenter thoracic radiotherapy trial, the AI type was a commercial deep-learning auto-segmentation model, iCurveE, for organs at risk. Safety was determined through contour-quality metrics that are directly relevant to normal-tissue sparing, especially volumetric Dice similarity coefficient and 95% Hausdorff distance, together with variability reduction across centers and clinicians. Efficacy was determined through contouring accuracy and time efficiency in real clinical workflows. In 500 patients across five centers, AI-assisted delineation achieved a mean volumetric Dice score of 0.902 versus 0.857 for manual delineation, improved mean HD95 from 8.01 mm to 5.20 mm, and reduced median contouring time from 55.0 minutes to 10.0 minutes, an 81.63% improvement. Because organ-at-risk delineation is foundational for dose constraints, that combination of more accurate contours and less variation indicates a meaningful safety improvement for patients rather than a purely administrative gain [7].

A parallel 2025 prostate online-adaptive radiotherapy study illustrates how AI began to move from contour-assistance toward real-time treatment adaptation. The AI type was an AI-integrated linear accelerator workflow with automatic organ-at-risk segmentation and online replanning. Safety was determined through dose-volume histogram metrics for bladder, bowel, and rectum, together with session-time feasibility; efficacy was determined primarily through target-coverage metrics. In 31 prostate cancer patients, adaptive treatment improved prostate target coverage, increasing PTV V95% by 10.4% and 11.8% in the two prostate groups and improving CTV D98% by 2.6% and 2.9%, without significantly worsening organ-at-risk dose indicators; median session times remained clinically workable at 25 minutes for prostate-only cases and 32 minutes for nodal cases. The key baseline lesson is that current adaptive AI is already improving dose conformity and workflow practicality, but the best-supported endpoints are still dosimetric rather than long-term survival endpoints [8].
