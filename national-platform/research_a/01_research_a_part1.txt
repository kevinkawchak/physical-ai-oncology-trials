U.S. Federal Branch-Operations Statutes and AI-Enabled Clinical Trials
Executive summary
The U.S. federal government’s three-branch structure is established primarily by the U.S. Constitution—Article I(Legislative), Article II (Executive), and Article III(Judicial). [1] Congress’s power to “make all Laws … necessary and proper” to carry constitutional powers into execution is a central constitutional basis for the extensive statutory “operating system” that governs the branches’ day-to-day functioning. [2]
A truly exhaustive inventory of “all U.S. laws” governing the branches’ current operations is not finite in practice because: (1) the U.S. Code contains thousands of permanent statutory provisions and (2) many core legislative operations are set by House/Senate rules adopted under Article I, Section 5 (not codified statutes). [3] This report therefore provides a structured, source-forward inventory of major constitutional provisions and the most operationally central federal statutes (with official titles and U.S.C./Statutes-at-Large citations where available from primary sources) that (a) establish branch institutions, (b) define permanently used operational processes (budgeting, rulemaking, oversight, records/publication), and/or (c) create core administrative bodies (e.g., GAO, CBO, AOUSC).
On AI in clinical trials (with oncology emphasis), there isno single, comprehensive “AI clinical trials statute”. Instead, regulatory control is a layered framework: (1) FDA’s medical product statutes and rules (FD&C Act, IND/IDE regulations, Part 11 electronic records), (2) human-subjects protection regimes (Common Rule, FDA human subject protection rules, IRBs), (3) privacy/security regimes (HIPAA; sometimes the Privacy Act for federal systems), and (4) targeted FDA guidances—including 2025–2026 guidances that explicitly address AI/ML credibility and AI-enabled device lifecycle management. [4] In oncology trials, these rules apply broadly but become especially salient where AI is used for imaging endpoints, digital pathology, patient selection/stratification, clinical decision support, decentralized data collection, and algorithm-driven safety signals. [5]
Constitutional framework for the three branches
Legislative branch
Establishing provisions (core): Article I vests “legislative Powers” in a Congress consisting of a Senate and House. [6]
Operationally decisive constitutional “controls”: Each House “may determine the Rules of its Proceedings” (making many operational rules extra-statutory). [7]
Operationally important constitutional timing: The Twentieth Amendment sets congressional terms ending/beginning at noon on January 3 and requires Congress to assemble (by default) at noon on January 3. [8]
Executive branch
Establishing provisions (core): Article II vests “executive Power” in a President. [9]
Operationally decisive constitutional “controls”: The Appointments Clause establishes Senate advice-and-consent for many officers and permits Congress to vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, courts, or department heads. [10]
Execution duty: Article II, Section 3 includes the “Take Care” duty and related presidential duties; it is a constitutional anchor for executive implementation and supervision of federal law.[11]
Judicial branch
Establishing provisions (core): Article III vests the “judicial Power” in one Supreme Court and such inferior courts as Congress may establish. [12]
Statutory implementation is structurally required: Article III establishes the Supreme Court but leaves major operational details—lower court creation, jurisdictional allocations, procedures—to Congress’s implementing statutes. This basic “constitutional design → statutory implementation” reality is reflected historically by the Judiciary Act tradition and modern Title 28 structure. [13]

Distinguishing “laws” from “internal rules” for legislative operations
A substantial portion of “how Congress operates” (floor procedure, committee procedure details, internal discipline mechanics, etc.) is set by each chamber’s rules adopted under Article I, Section 5—meaning many operational provisions are not codified as U.S. Code statutes. [46] This is a core reason that a statutory-only list cannot fully describe legislative “current operations.”
Cross-branch governance statutes that materially affect operations
Transparency, recordkeeping, and public notice as operational infrastructure
Publication and recordkeeping statutes are key to modern federal operations because they define how law becomes knowable and auditable:
Federal Register publication requirements for presidential proclamations/executive orders (where applicable) are anchored in 44 U.S.C. § 1505. [25]
Executive agency record creation/preservation obligations are codified in 44 U.S.C. § 3101, structuring how agencies document decisions and protect legal rights. [31]
Presidential record definitions for coverage are set in 44 U.S.C. § 2201. [30]
Public access to executive information is operationalized through FOIA (5 U.S.C. § 552). [47]
Federal privacy/data governance for “systems of records” is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552a, including criminal penalties for some willful conduct. [48]
Budget execution constraints
Statutory fiscal controls constrain operations of all branches and agencies. The Antideficiency Act prohibits obligations/expenditures beyond appropriations and certain pre-appropriation obligations. [34]
Ethics and disclosure across branches
The Ethics in Government Act framework is an example of an operational statute spanning branches (especially disclosure and ethics administration), with codified compilation in the U.S. Code Appendix and government publishing sources. [49]
AI in clinical trials and oncology
How “AI in trials” is regulated in practice
AI use in clinical trials is regulated primarily through what the AI is doing (e.g., producing evidence for regulatory decisions, performing a medical-device function, processing regulated electronic records, influencing participant safety) rather than by an “AI clinical trials” statute.
A useful organizing distinction (reflected in FDA guidance) is:
AI used to generate information/data intended to support regulatory decision-making for drugs/biologics(model credibility, transparency, drift, lifecycle maintenance). [50]
AI embedded in, or functioning as, a medical device software function (SaMD/AI-enabled device software; lifecycle management; PCCPs). [51]
AI used as part of trial conduct and data systems(electronic records/signatures, computerized systems, DHTs, decentralized elements). [52]
AI as human-subjects research and data risk (Common Rule, IRB review, privacy/confidentiality, identifiability).[53]
AI and oncology clinical trials: why oncology is a high-frequency “AI use case”
The FDA Oncology Center of Excellence[54] describes an Oncology AI Program begun in 2023 and links AI-related FDA guidances used in oncology regulatory contexts. [55]In oncology trials specifically, AI is frequently implicated because endpoints and eligibility often rely on imaging (CT/MRI/PET), pathology, complex biomarker patterns, and longitudinal EHR-derived outcomes, all of which are common AI input modalities and are risk-sensitive from a bias, validation, and data integrity standpoint (as reflected in federal AI/human-subjects oversight discussions). [56]

Key “operational provisions” in the main AI-specific FDA drug/biologic guidance (Jan 2025 draft)
FDA’s draft guidance on AI for drug/biologic regulatory decision-making is operationally notable for three reasons:
1) Scope boundaries: It applies when AI models are used “to produce information or data to support regulatory decision-making regarding safety, effectiveness, or quality for drugs,” and explicitly excludes AI used only for operational efficiencies that do not affect patient safety, drug quality, or reliability of nonclinical/clinical study results.[50] 2) Credibility framework: It defines a 7-step risk-based credibility assessment process (define question of interest; define context of use; assess model risk; plan; execute; document; determine adequacy). [50] 3) Lifecycle maintenance and drift: It highlights data drift and performance change over time and recommends risk-based lifecycle monitoring, with reporting to FDA “in accordance with regulatory requirements” when changes impact performance in certain contexts. [50]
Practical compliance synthesis for oncology trials using AI
In oncology trials, “compliance” is usually about building a defensible chain from context of use → validation/credibility → auditability → human-subjects protection:
If AI outputs will be used as evidence for FDA decisions about safety/effectiveness/quality, expect to document the model’s context of use, risk, and credibility assessmentconsistent with FDA’s draft framework even if the guidance is nonbinding. [143]
If AI functions as a regulated device, the trial may implicate IDE and device marketing pathways, and lifecycle management/PCCP expectations become central to “what changes are allowed and how they are controlled.” [144]
If AI relies on decentralized/DHT data, the “weak link” is often data attribution, metadata integrity, and Part 11 boundaries—FDA’s electronic systems Q&A explicitly notes that once electronic records enter a sponsor’s EDC system, FDA intends to assess Part 11 compliance, and emphasizes sponsor responsibility for data integrity in submissions. [81]
If AI training/validation relies on secondary data and exemptions, SACHRP’s AI recommendations emphasize that many studies can be technically compliant yet still leave subjects or groups exposed to foreseeable harms, and it flags identifiability and bias as persistent gaps. [89]
