Three days.

That is how long it took to finish the paper attached to this post: fifty-three
pages, twenty-five figures, twenty-five tables, eleven sections. One author. One
prompt.

The paper is not about the paper. It is about what one person can now put in
front of a funder for pancreatic cancer, and how fast.

Here is the record it documents:

- An Investigational New Drug application, twelve modules under 21 CFR 312,
  assembled in four days.
- Two clinical trial protocols. A Phase 1 first-in-human study at n = 18 behind
  a simulation gate of at least 1,000 runs across two frameworks, and a Phase 2
  randomized study at n = 220 across eight centers.
- Five versions of a Federal bill with four companion documents, deposited in
  eleven days.
- Fourteen funding artifacts, including a $1,606,000 small-business ask against
  a $3,500,000 five-year direct program.

The prior system produces those documents on a month scale, through teams, with
peer review arriving after the work is finished. This one produces them on a one
to four day scale, through one person, with review arriving during development.

The part people argue with is the review. So the paper measures it instead of
asserting it.

A best-case human peer review round is seven to eight weeks. A typical journal
processing time is several months. Three model reviewers from three different
manufacturers - Anthropic producing, OpenAI as first independent reviewer,
Google as second - read one frozen artifact and return three independent reports
the same day. A human principal investigator holds final authority, and two of
the five ways a disagreement can resolve stop the artifact rather than passing
it.

Now the number I keep coming back to.

US pancreatic cancer deaths ran about 51,980 in 2025. That is roughly 142 people
a day. One best-case review round, seven to eight weeks, spans about 7,400 of
them.

Peer review does not cause those deaths. The disease does. But when the delay is
measured against a disease that kills 142 Americans a day, review latency stops
being an administrative preference and becomes a quantity worth optimizing.

There is a cost side too. List article processing charges commonly run $2,000 to
$6,000 per paper, above $10,000 at some flagship titles - paid by the researcher,
to the journal, for a page on the journal's own site. The workflow this method
came from cost $35 in total inference across a fourteen-day study.

For anyone building in this space, the method is the deliverable. The master
prompt is filed verbatim. The eight-stage schedule is filed. All twenty-five
figures are machine-readable specifications with absolute coordinates, not
images, so the next paper reads them instead of redrawing them. Every file was
committed the moment it was written, so the branch history is the production
record rather than a report about it.

What I still cannot produce by writing: a qualified site, an operating room, a
pathology service, an IRB of record, a drug supply agreement, a device acceptance
certificate, a clinical trial agreement. Seven institutional instruments. That is
the entire remaining gap, and it is the reason this paper is the last document in
the sequence rather than the next one in it.

If you work on pancreatic cancer trials, physical AI in the operating room, or
research funding, the paper is attached. I would rather hear where it is wrong
than hear nothing.
