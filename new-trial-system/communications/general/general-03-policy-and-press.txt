One author, one prompt, three days: what it means for Federal AI and cancer policy

Between September 2025 and mid-2026 the Federal government committed to using
artificial intelligence against cancer in an unusually direct way. Executive
Order 14355 tasked an AI-driven approach to pediatric cancer. The Childhood
Cancer Data Initiative budget doubled from $50 million to $100 million. The
Genesis Mission launched with more than $1.2 billion obligated and planned across
fiscal 2026 and 2027. The Department of Health and Human Services launched a
department-wide clinical trials effort in June 2026, and the National Cancer
Institute began working with cancer centers to streamline trial activation.

Every one of those actions supplies capability: compute, data, agency review
capacity, faster site activation. None of them supplies a method for producing
the documents a sponsor must file. That gap is what the attached paper closes,
and it closes it with a worked example rather than a proposal.

The measured claims, each checkable against a deposited artifact:

- An Investigational New Drug application, twelve modules under 21 CFR 312,
  assembled in four days by one author.
- Two clinical trial protocols: a Phase 1 first-in-human study at n = 18 behind
  a simulation gate of at least 1,000 runs across two frameworks, and a Phase 2
  randomized study at n = 220 across eight centers.
- Five versions of a Federal bill with four companion documents, in eleven days.
- Fourteen funding artifacts, including a $1,606,000 small-business ask against a
  $3,500,000 five-year direct program.
- A virtual trial projected at $36,330 against an industry benchmark above
  $120,000 per run.
- This paper, fifty-three pages with twenty-five figures and twenty-five tables,
  finished in three days.

The finding a policy reader should take away is about the review layer rather
than the production layer. A best-case human peer review round is seven to eight
weeks; a typical journal processing time is several months. One author deposited
more than thirty works in 2026. A review regime sized for two to six works a
year is not a constraint at thirty, it is an exclusion, and the work does not
enter it. The paper's argument is that the two systems are incompatible rather
than merely different, and that the incompatibility is arithmetic rather than
attitude.

The cost structure is worth a line of its own. List article processing charges
commonly run $2,000 to $6,000 per article and exceed $10,000 at several flagship
titles. The researcher pays the journal, and the journal supplies a page on its
own site carrying the researcher's work. The workflow this review method came
from cost $35 in total inference across a fourteen-day study.

Two things this work does not say, and both should survive any summary of it.
First, model peer review is not regulatory review; it does not replace an
institutional review board, an FDA information request, or a data and safety
monitoring board, and the paper states that limit in its own words. Second, the
bill discussed in the paper is an author-drafted legislative proposal. It has not
been introduced and is not pending before Congress.
