What an investigator should check first in this paper, and what to be skeptical of

The attached paper reports an IND assembled in four days, two protocols, five
bill versions with four companion documents in eleven days, and fourteen funding
artifacts, produced by one author directing a language model. If your reaction is
that a four-day IND sounds like a four-day IND, that is the right reaction, and
the paper is written to be falsified rather than admired. Four checks are
available to you without any special access.

First, open Figure 8. It crosswalks each codified 21 CFR 312.23 content item to
the named section file that satisfies it and to the repository path where that
file is deposited. Pick any content item. The named file either satisfies it or
it does not.

Second, take any of the 52 distinct 21 CFR cross-references in the dossier and
read the cited paragraph. The dossier either states the requirement correctly or
it does not.

Third, take any number that appears in more than one module: the day-30 safety
window, the 3 N per-arm force cap, the n = 18 ceiling. Check that every
occurrence agrees. Internal inconsistency across modules is the characteristic
failure of fast document production, and it is the thing worth hunting for.

Fourth, check the four-day production claim itself against the commit history,
which records when each module was written and pushed. The claim is checkable
because the artifacts were deposited as they were produced rather than described
afterward.

On the clinical substance, the design is conservative rather than aggressive.
Phase 1 is n = 18 on a 3+3 escalation, and it sits behind a Phase 0 gate
requiring at least 1,000 simulations across two frameworks before any patient
contact. Phase 2 is n = 220 across eight high-volume centers, randomized 1:1,
targeting a hazard ratio of 0.60 with about 140 progression-free survival events
for 85 percent power. The human baseline is the 2025 Dutch nationwide cohort of
1,000 robotic pancreaticoduodenectomies: conversion 10.1 percent, Clavien-Dindo
grade III or higher 41.3 percent, ISGPS grade B or C fistula 24.4 percent, 30-day
mortality 3.9 percent. The language model's output is advisory. The surgeon
decides. That boundary is a protocol provision, not a preference.

What the paper does not claim, and this matters more than anything it does claim:
model peer review is not regulatory review, is not clinical validation, and does
not establish correctness by agreement. Nothing in this system substitutes for an
institutional review board, an FDA information request, or a data and safety
monitoring board. Three models agreeing produces three reports, not a proof,
which is why two of the five ways a reviewer disagreement can resolve stop the
artifact rather than passing it.

What is missing is institutional, not documentary: a qualified site, an operating
room, a pathology service able to adjudicate margin status at day 90, an
institutional review board of record, a drug supply agreement, a device
acceptance certificate, and a clinical trial agreement. If you hold any of those
and think the design is worth a conversation, I would like to have it.
