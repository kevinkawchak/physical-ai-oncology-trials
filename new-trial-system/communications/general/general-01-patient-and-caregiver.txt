Why a paper about paperwork matters to someone with pancreatic cancer

Before any new pancreatic cancer treatment can be tried in a person, somebody has
to write an enormous amount of documentation. A dossier for the Food and Drug
Administration. A protocol saying exactly who can join the study, what dose they
get, and what stops the study. A funding application. Historically this takes
teams of people many months, and then the scientific write-up goes to a journal
where two or three reviewers read it over another seven to eight weeks at best,
often several months.

The attached paper documents a different way of doing that part. One person,
working with an AI system, produced the Food and Drug Administration dossier in
four days, two full study protocols, and fourteen funding documents. The paper
itself, with fifty diagrams and tables, was finished in three days.

Here is why the speed is worth caring about, stated carefully so it is not
oversold. Pancreatic cancer killed about 51,980 people in the United States in
2025. That is about 142 people a day. Every seven to eight weeks of waiting on a
review round is about 7,400 people. Nobody dies because a reviewer was slow, and
this paper does not claim that. What it does claim is simpler: when a disease
kills 142 Americans a day, the months spent waiting are not a neutral
administrative cost, and they are worth removing where they can be removed
safely.

What has not changed, and should not:

- No patient is treated based on anything an AI decides. In the study design, the
  computer system gives advice and a surgeon decides. That boundary is written
  into the protocol.
- The Food and Drug Administration still reviews the dossier. An ethics board
  still approves the study. An independent safety board still monitors it. None
  of that is replaced by anything in this paper, and the paper says so directly.
- Every participant in the planned study can be told in plain language what the
  system does, and can opt out of the AI-assisted portion without losing access
  to the study.

What is still missing is not writing. It is a hospital, an operating room, a
pathology laboratory, an ethics board of record, and agreements for the drug and
the equipment. Those take people and institutions saying yes. The documents are
finished and waiting.

If you want to read the details, the paper is attached. If you would rather ask a
question than read fifty-three pages, ask it. I would rather answer than be
misunderstood.
