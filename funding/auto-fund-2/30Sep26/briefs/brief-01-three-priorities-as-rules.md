# Three Priorities, Stated as Operating Rules

**ChemicalQDevice, San Diego.** Kevin Kawchak, CEO.
For any reader of the September 12, 2026 message. About 700 words.

---

## The message

On September 12, 2026 the chief executive wrote to the President:

> Dear Mr. Trump,
>
> I am thankful for some humbling life circumstances leading to priority changes for myself and the startup which include:
> 1) Obedience over hard work and responsibilities.
> 2) Adapted to existing authority, order, and structure.
> 3) Acclimated to a teamwork based early-rise, scheduled routine.
>
> Sincerely,
> CEO Kevin Kawchak
> ChemicalQDevice
> September 12, 2026

Several communications from the White House followed. Their text is not in this
repository and is not paraphrased here.

## Why each priority needs a rule

A priority stated in a letter is a promise. A promise a counterparty cannot
check is worth what the counterparty decides to believe, and a small company
cannot afford to depend on belief. Each priority below is therefore restated as
a rule, with the behavior that shows the rule is being kept.

| Priority | Operating rule | Observable evidence |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Obedience over hard work and responsibilities | When an office that owns a rule answers, the company follows the answer before doing more of its own work | Each letter this block sends asks the owner of the rule first, and waits |
| Adapted to existing authority, order, and structure | The company takes the role an existing structure offers, even when it is smaller than the role it asked for | Day 3's letters to the Genesis Mission, Moores and CTEP each ask for a contributor's place |
| Acclimated to a teamwork-based early-rise, scheduled routine | The working day starts at 5:00 a.m. Pacific and runs to a fixed timetable that every team member keeps | Correspondence goes in the 6:00 to 8:00 a.m. window, which is Washington's morning |

## Rule 1 in practice

The CTEP reply in the first block is the model. The company asked a question in
one shape, the branch chief answered that the shape was wrong, and the company
changed its approach rather than arguing. Every letter in this block is built
the same way: it asks the office that owns the rule, it states what the company
will do with the answer, and it does not do that work before the answer comes.

| Rule owner | Question asked this block | What the company does while it waits |
|:--|:--|:--|
| NIH SBIR program office | How contingent personnel are named | Names no one |
| E-Verify Contact Center | The sequence of verification | Does not enroll |
| SEC San Francisco office | Venue and form of record | Makes no public claim about the office |
| FDA Oncology AI Program | Which venue owns the clinical question | Describes no FDA position |
| NSF SBIR program | Pilot eligibility | Files only the Project Pitch the rules allow |

## Rule 2 in practice

Rule 2 is the harder one, because it asks the company to accept less than it
asked for. The first block wrote to CTEP as a company bringing a concept; the
answer was that concepts belong to investigators. This block writes to Moores
offering the software component to an investigator, and to the Genesis Mission
offering to serve on a laboratory's team. Neither letter proposes that the
company lead.

## Rule 3 in practice

An early start is not a virtue in itself. It is useful because the offices this
company writes to are three hours ahead: 6:00 a.m. in La Jolla is 9:00 a.m. in
Washington. A letter sent in that window arrives at the start of a federal
working day, and a reply sent before 2:00 p.m. Pacific can be answered the same
day. The full weekday is in `brief-03-the-early-rise-weekday.md`.

The word "teamwork" in the third priority matters as much as the schedule. The
company has one member today and two unsolicited applicants. Each reply on day 1
told the applicant about the schedule before anything else about the role, so
that the first people to join are the first people to keep it.

## What the rules are not

They are not a claim that any office has approved the company's conduct, that
the White House has endorsed it, or that obedience to an office's answer implies
agreement with it in every case. Where the company disagrees with an answer, it
says so once, in writing, to the office that gave it, and then follows it.
