\section{The Reserve on Two Branches}

A funder or a holder reading a small business proposal reasonably asks how that
business holds the cash it already has. Day 1 answered it for a nine-month
horizon \cite{treasuryauctions2026}. This day answers what changes if a financing
is taken, and what stays exactly as it is if one is not.

The instruction is conditional rather than new. The reserve's horizon is a
function of how long the company has to operate on its own capital, a financing
changes that horizon, and re-cutting a ladder against a guess is the exact cost a
ladder exists to avoid.

Branch A is the unfinanced case and is the day 1 instruction unchanged: a
nine-month horizon, four rungs, and no action beyond letting each maturity fall
to the sweep. Branch B is the financed case at a twenty-four-month horizon, with
six rungs rather than four, because a two-year horizon spanned by four rungs
leaves six-month gaps between maturities and a company's cash needs do not arrive
on a six-month grid.

\subsection{What stays excluded on both branches}

No margin, no options, no securities lending, no prime money market fund, no
corporate credit of any maturity, no equity, and no auto-reinvestment on any
rung. A financing does not change what a research company's reserve is for. It
changes how long the reserve has to last, which is a maturity question rather
than a credit question.

\subsection{Three sources of money, three treatments}

Subscription funds, if any arrive, are held in a separate account under the same
entity and are not commingled with the operating reserve; they sit in a
government sweep and are drawn against the seven proceeds lines of \S1 rather
than laddered, because a maturity that has to be broken is worse than a lower
yield. Federal award funds, if any arrive, are held and drawn under the award's
own terms and are mixed with neither \cite{movein2026}. The separation is
established before the first dollar rather than reconstructed afterward, which is
what the account letter of this day is for.
